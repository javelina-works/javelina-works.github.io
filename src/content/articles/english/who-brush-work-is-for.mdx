Not long ago I was telling a man about what we do: flying ranches, counting brush plant by plant, treating the ones that need to go and coming back to check. He listened, then asked the fair question. "Why is it important? Why would somebody want this?"

The honest answer is that it depends on who owns the land. A cow-calf rancher, a new owner from the city, a land trust and a state wildlife area all want something different out of the same pasture. This article goes through them one at a time: what each wants, what the evidence says, and what a survey, treatment and monitoring service does for them. If you are wondering whether this is for you, or for someone you know, start here.

## Cow-calf ranchers: grass, stocking rate and drought

For a working ranch, brush is a grass problem. When a large survey asked 1,058 Texas landowners why they wanted less brush, more forage and water conservation were the top two answers.[^1] Out here, the water mostly stays in the soil and grows grass, so for a rancher the two come down to the same thing.

The forage response is real. In a north Texas study, cutting mesquite from 30 percent cover to 10 percent changed carrying capacity from about 30 acres per animal unit to about 14.[^2] That was higher-rainfall clay loam, and gains in the Trans-Pecos are smaller. The [cattle profitability article](/articles/brush-control-cattle-profitability/) goes through what that means in pounds of calf. Even modest gains matter in a dry year, because grass you grow is hay you do not have to buy.

Timing matters more than most people expect. Researchers working on redberry juniper in west-central Texas found grass production falls fastest once cover passes the high teens, and advised treating before juniper cover reaches about 12 percent.[^3] Scattered young plants are cheap to treat one at a time. A thick stand is not.

What a service does for a rancher: a [survey](/services/survey-work/) maps where the brush actually is, so the money goes to the deep-soil flats where grass will answer. [Treatment](/services/brush-targeting/) catches the stand while it is still scattered. [Monitoring](/services/monitoring/) shows what died, which also helps when a cost-share program wants proof the work was done. If you want to run your own numbers, the [brush ROI calculator](/tools/brush-roi/) does that with your costs and your country.

## Newer, absentee and recreational owners

Texas ranch land is changing hands. Texas A&M's Natural Resources Institute reports that 33 percent of Texas landowners are absentee, 32 percent have less than ten years of experience, and 41 percent of rural landowners are over 65. About 56 million acres face transfer to the next generation, and the state loses more than 1,000 acres of working land a day.[^4] The same work notes that newer owners have different goals and are less tied to the land.[^4]

Those owners tend to want something other than maximum grass. Researchers on the Edwards Plateau found land being bought by people who do not depend on land income and "may be more tolerant of brush."[^5] And a Junction appraiser wrote in 1997 that in his market, properties with no woody vegetation were in the least demand and sold for the lowest prices, while heavily brushed places were discounted for the cost of opening them up.[^6] That is one appraiser's view from another region, not a study, but it matches what these buyers say they want.

For this owner the goal is the right amount of brush in the right places: mule deer and quail cover, shade trees, a view. Texas Parks and Wildlife puts it plainly: "inadequate cover can be just as detrimental as too much brush," and individual plant treatment gives "the ultimate in selectivity."[^7] The [how much brush to keep](/articles/how-much-brush-to-keep/) article covers the targets.

What a service does here: most of these owners have no crew and no equipment, and many are not on the place often. A plant-by-plant map lets them decide on screen what stays and what goes, and a repeat flight shows them what happened while they were away. Keeping oaks takes setbacks, not promises, because the hexazinone label warns that desirable trees can take it up through roots reaching into treated ground.[^8]

## Owners keeping a wildlife valuation

Many owners keep their agricultural property tax valuation by switching to wildlife management use. To qualify, the owner carries out at least three of seven listed practices every year under a plan filed with the appraisal district. TPWD is clear that it is a valuation, not an exemption, and the appraisal district decides.[^9]

Brush management counts under habitat control. In the Trans-Pecos, it has to treat at least 10 percent of the plan area or 100 acres a year, whichever is smaller, and it includes "retaining the proper kind, amount, and distribution of woody cover."[^10] On a 640-acre plan, that is 64 acres every year.

That is what makes it a recurring job rather than a one-time project. A survey sets the baseline, each year's treatment works through the next block, and the per-plant logs give the owner a clean record of acres, dates and what was treated. The [wildlife valuation article](/articles/wildlife-valuation-brush-management/) goes into the details.

## Land trusts and easement holders

A lot of Davis Mountains country is under conservation protection. The Nature Conservancy describes its Davis Mountains Preserve as 32,844 acres, with later purchases and easements on neighboring land bringing protection to nearly 110,000 acres in the region.[^11] The Texas Agricultural Land Trust handles easement agreements under a federal partnership funding easements and grassland restoration in the Trans-Pecos.[^12] The state's Texas Farm & Ranch Lands Conservation Program helps holders prepare a baseline report on an easement's conservation values and reviews annual monitoring reports.[^13]

Those are examples of who carries the obligation, and the obligation is documentation. An easement holder has to show, year after year, that the grassland is still grassland and nothing was cleared that should not have been.

What a service does for them: a georeferenced woody-cover baseline, and a repeat flight that shows change plant by plant. That is useful even when nothing is treated at all. Treatment only enters the picture where the management plan calls for restoring grassland. Our [monitoring service](/services/monitoring/) is built for that kind of record.

## Conservation partnerships and public land managers

Some of the biggest brush work in this region is done for habitat. Sul Ross's Borderlands Research Institute reports that Chihuahuan Desert grasslands are "threatened by shrub encroachment," and that grassland birds wintering there are declining faster than elsewhere.[^14] The federal partnership mentioned above commits BRI to "actively monitor the results" of its restoration projects.[^12] TPWD's Elephant Mountain Wildlife Management Area south of Alpine is run as a research and demonstration area, with restoring wildlife habitat as its first goal.[^15]

Across the line in New Mexico, the Bureau of Land Management and local conservation districts drop tebuthiuron pellets from low-flying planes on creosote and tarbush, a use that dates to the early 1980s.[^16] That is a different product from the hexazinone we use, but the same idea: a soil-active pellet that works through the roots. On a 2017 aerial mesquite treatment, BLM said it expected an average root kill of only about 40 percent.[^17]

That number is the case for measuring. If a broadcast treatment is expected to kill four plants in ten, someone has to count which four. BRI's own report found that after a 4,000-acre broadcast treatment in West Texas, shrubland birds declined but grassland birds did not increase.[^14] Outcomes are not automatic. For these managers, the service is pre- and post-treatment counts, and selective work in mixed stands, near riparian ground or around sensitive sites where broadcasting is the wrong tool.

## People worried about fire near homes and roads

The 2011 Rock House fire burned about 314,000 acres and destroyed 24 homes in Fort Davis.[^18][^19] Brush did not start it, and no one can honestly say less brush would have stopped it. Grass carries fire too.

What brush does change is how fire behaves close in. Extension guidance focuses on defensible space within about 100 to 200 feet of structures.[^20] A dense cedar slope above a house is a different problem than open grass. For these owners, a survey is a fuel inventory around houses, barns, roads and power lines, and treatment thins what sits closest. The [wildfire article](/articles/brush-and-wildfire-davis-mountains/) covers what the research does and does not support.

Other land users, like pipeline and utility rights-of-way, may also need vegetation records some day, but we have little evidence yet that brush work is what they need.

## Where it is not the right fit

Plant-by-plant work is not always the best answer.

On broad, dense stands, per-plant cost climbs with every plant. Extension cost guidance for mesquite says that once individual plant treatment gets expensive enough, broadcast herbicide or mechanical work is worth a look.[^21] If the whole pasture is closed in, a dozer or an airplane may be cheaper, and we will say so.

If the main reason is water yield, this country is a hard place to get it. Rangeland hydrologist Brad Wilcox found little potential to increase streamflow where rainfall is under about 20 inches a year.[^22] Fort Davis averages about 16 inches, and Marfa and Alpine get less.[^23] Here the water brush stops using mostly stays in the soil and grows grass. The [brush and water article](/articles/brush-and-water-dry-country/) explains why.

And if the place is already at the brush cover you want, the right move is to leave it alone and check it now and then.
