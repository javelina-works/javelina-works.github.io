Individual plant treatment, usually shortened to IPT, is the opposite of clearing a pasture. You find each mesquite or cedar you want gone, you treat that plant, and you leave the rest alone. On a lot of West Texas country it is the cheapest way to hold the line, and the only method that lets you decide, tree by tree, what stays. If you are still deciding whether to treat at all, start with [why manage brush](/articles/why-manage-brush/). Cedar here means what it means on the ranch: redberry juniper and Ashe juniper both.

## What individual plant treatment is

Texas A&M's herbicide guide for rangeland draws the line plainly: individual plant treatments are suited to thin stands of brush and to selective control, and broadcast treatments are for dense stands.[^1] The Extension rule of thumb is fewer than 200 stems per acre for IPT.[^2]

There are four main ways to treat a plant on its own. Leaf spray wets every leaf with a dilute herbicide mix until the foliage glistens but does not drip.[^3] Stem spray puts triclopyr in diesel or basal oil on the lower 12 inches of each trunk, 15 percent for smooth bark under 1.5 inches and 25 percent for larger stems.[^3] Cut stump means sawing the plant off and spraying what is left; a redberry cedar stump gets 4 percent Tordon 22K, while Ashe juniper (blueberry cedar) needs no spray once it is cut.[^4] Soil-applied treatment puts undiluted hexazinone on the ground under the plant, as a liquid or a pellet, and lets rain carry it to the roots; for cedar under 3 feet the rate is 2 milliliters per 3 feet of height or canopy, placed midway between the stem and the canopy edge.[^5]

## Where the numbers come from

Most of what is known about IPT in Texas comes out of one program. In the 1990s Allan McGinty and Darrell Ueckert at the Texas A&M center in San Angelo built Brush Busters, a joint Extension and Experiment Station effort to put do-it-yourself, individual plant methods for mesquite, cedar and pricklypear into ranchers' hands.[^6]

A 1999 survey of county Extension agents put more than 405,000 hectares, about a million acres, under Brush Busters treatment between 1995 and 1998, 78 percent of it mesquite, and still less than 7 percent of the area the agents thought was treatable that way.[^7] A 2000 survey of 1,058 landowners in 48 counties found they preferred individual herbicide treatments for mesquite and pricklypear and ranked them cheaper than mechanical or aerial work.[^8]

The leaflets carry the same efficacy claim for all three targets: 76 to 100 percent root kill under the right conditions, or as the summary pages put it, more than seven of ten plants treated.[^3][^9][^10][^11]

## You keep what you want

This is the advantage no broadcast method can offer. The mesquite page says it directly: you keep the mesquites and the other shrubs and trees you want, and get rid of the ones you don't.[^11] Dale Rollins' guidance for sculpting brush around wildlife keeps clearings under 400 yards across for deer, with 200 preferred, and under 100 yards for quail, with mottes and stringers of brush left standing.[^12]

Broadcast spraying has collateral cost even with the newer chemistry. A three-year aerial trial in north Texas found that a clopyralid and aminopyralid mix was tolerated by lotebush and hackberry, but adding picloram made hackberry susceptible, worse under drought, and the authors recommend checking the nontarget woody species on a pasture before broadcast spraying it.[^13]

IPT also moves no dirt. The NRCS brush management standard tells planners to pick the method with the least soil disturbance where erosion potential is high.[^14] The San Angelo center's review of mechanical jobs found that raking and stacking grubbed brush smooths the surface and increases runoff, and that removing the parent cedar trees often sets off an explosion of new seedlings across the pasture.[^15]

## How well it works, plant by plant

For cedar with hexazinone pellets, McGinty ran plots in five counties in the summer of 2001 and read them two years later. Root kill averaged 84 percent at one pellet per 3 feet of height and 94 percent at two, against 88 and 96 percent for the equivalent liquid rates.[^16] The range matters as much as the average: the Lampasas site came in at 47 percent on the low rate while the Mills County site hit 100 at both.[^16] The current state guide rates hexazinone on redberry juniper under 6 feet very high, meaning 76 to 100 percent, and drops it to high on taller trees even at double the dose.[^1]

For seedlings, the old work still stands. Ueckert and Whisenant found that cutting or hand grubbing redberry juniper seedlings about 34 centimeters tall gave complete control at the lowest cost, that 0.5 percent foliar sprays also gave complete control, and that pelleted products did not.[^17]

For pricklypear, Extension trials in nine counties from 2004 to 2006 found 76 to 78 percent of treated colonies had at least 75 percent pad mortality two years out, with no difference among the four herbicides tested but a real difference by season: 86 percent for summer treatments against 71 percent for winter.[^18]

For comparison, aerial spraying of mesquite rarely produces more than 80 percent root kill, and grubbing kills what it grubs but typically misses 40 to 100 small plants per acre.[^15]

## Less herbicide on the ground

A spray plane puts product on every acre whether there is a mesquite on it or not. The state guide calls for a minimum of 4 gallons of total spray volume per acre for aerial broadcast and 10 to 25 gallons for ground broadcast.[^1] IPT puts a 1 percent mix on the plant and nothing between plants.[^3][^9] The Brush Busters authors noted that the quantity of herbicide used can often be reduced compared with broadcast treatment, with most sites running well below label maximums.[^6]

Soil-applied hexazinone is the exception. It is non-selective where it lands, grass dies in the treated spot and takes two to three years to recover, and the label keeps you three times the dripline distance from any tree you want to keep and under 600 pellets per acre per season.[^5][^19]

## What it costs, and when the math flips

Every Brush Busters leaflet says the same thing about cost: it rises rapidly as the brush gets bigger and denser.[^3] Broadcast costs are per acre; IPT costs are per plant.

The early Brush Busters numbers, at 1990s prices, put leaf spray at 7.4 to 18.7 cents a plant and soil spot spray at 5.1 to 7.5 cents, which worked out to $9.60 to $35 an acre for leaf spray and $9.58 to $16.90 for soil treatment, against $18 to $80 an acre for mechanical work.[^6] The Extension cost sheet for small cedar, at 1998 prices and $6 an hour labor, estimates about $13 an acre at 100 cedars per acre, and says that if your estimate greatly exceeds $30 an acre you should consider fire or a mechanical treatment instead.[^20] For scale, the north Texas aerial recommendation of that era, clopyralid plus triclopyr at 0.25 pounds of each, cost $23 an acre.[^21]

Where the crossover sits depends on who you ask. Ueckert put IPT at its best below 200 plants per acre on cedar no taller than about 3 feet.[^22] New Mexico State's mesquite guide says fewer than 100 plants per acre or under 5 percent canopy cover.[^23] The San Angelo center's 2004 review says Brush Busters methods may be cost effective against broadcast at 300 to 400 plants per acre, or higher for some species.[^15] Our [ballpark tool](/tools/ballpark/) will give you a rough feel for your own place.

The cheapest IPT is the one you do on regrowth. Ueckert worked out that keeping cedar seedlings down by hand on a 6 year cycle costs about $1.03 an acre a year, or 77 cents on an 8 year cycle.[^22] The San Angelo review puts it bluntly: without follow-up, an initial treatment has a 20 to 30 year life; with low-cost follow-up started 3 to 5 years after the first job, it lasts indefinitely.[^15] The NRCS standard says the same in its maintenance section: expect some regrowth, and spot treat individual plants while they are small and most vulnerable.[^14]

## Timing and moisture matter more than the product

Mesquite leaf spray goes on after the leaves turn from pea green to a uniform dark green and the soil at 12 to 18 inches has reached 75 degrees, and it runs through July 31 in East and South Texas and into September elsewhere.[^3][^24] Do not spray after rain has pushed new growth at the tips, when the leaves are wet, or when hail or insects have damaged the foliage, and give a top-killed tree two full growing seasons first.[^3] Stem spray works any time of year, best in the growing season.[^25] Cedar leaf spray is a late spring to summer job on plants under 3 feet.[^9]

Soil-applied hexazinone runs on rain, not the calendar. The Extension method says late winter to mid spring, ideally ahead of expected rainfall, because rain is what activates the product.[^5] The pellet label wants moist soil at application and a quarter to half an inch of rain within two weeks, with plants responding three to six weeks after enough rain has carried the herbicide into the root zone.[^19] It is not for clay soils or poorly drained sites; coarse soils give the best results.[^1] Pricklypear can be sprayed year round, but the nine-county data favor summer.[^18]

## Labor is the cost, which is why precision matters

Strip IPT down and there are two inputs: herbicide and hours. The Extension cost sheet asks for a labor rate, a plant count and plant size, and little else.[^20] The San Angelo review calls IPT personnel-intensive, with the most variable results of any method unless it is supervised, and reports that only half of the chemical IPT jobs it surveyed on the North Concho project were judged successful, mostly because of application error and marginal conditions.[^15]

That is why the hardware keeps changing, from low energy grubbers to hydraulic shears with a spray nozzle to aircraft. New Mexico State's current juniper guide lists helicopters, fixed-wing planes and emerging unmanned aircraft among the ways to put a treatment on an individual tree.[^15][^4][^26]

## Cost-share: NRCS 314 and EQIP

NRCS Conservation Practice Standard 314, Brush Management, defines the practice as the management or removal of woody plants, lists wildlife habitat and forage among its purposes, and requires that treatment achieve control of the target species and protection of desired species.[^14] Texas NRCS payment schedules pay brush management by scenario, and individual plant treatment is one of them, split into a low class of 50 to 200 plants per acre and a high class of 201 to 400, alongside broadcast and mechanical classes by canopy cover.[^27] In July 2025 Texas NRCS proposed adding 314 to its top ten priority practices for fiscal 2026 EQIP.[^28] [Get in touch](/contact/) if you want help putting a survey map in front of your field office.

## Where IPT falls short

Density is the first limit, and it is a hard one. The methods were built for small plants: mesquite leaf spray for trees under 6 feet, cedar leaf and soil treatments for plants under 3 feet.[^3][^29] The New Mexico guide places individual treatments in the early stages of encroachment, when seedlings and juveniles under about 5 feet or 6 years old are the focus.[^26]

Resprouting is the second. Redberry cedar sprouts from the base, so topping it does not control plants older than 8 to 10 years, and seedlings have to be grubbed below the basal knob at or just under the soil surface.[^30] Ashe juniper does not resprout and dies when cut.[^22]

Patience is the third. Pricklypear can take two or three years to die after a pad spray.[^10] And no method, this one included, is a one-time job; the leaflet tells you to monitor every year for new plants, because livestock and wildlife keep spreading the seed.[^3]

## What this means for your place

If your brush is still scattered, IPT is probably the cheapest thing you can do and the only thing that lets you keep the trees you like. If it is already a thicket, something bigger goes first and IPT is the maintenance plan that makes the big job last. Either way the decision starts with a count.
