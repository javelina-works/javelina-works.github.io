The biggest cost-share program you are likely to use for brush work is the federal Environmental Quality Incentives Program, EQIP, which pays for it under NRCS Practice 314, Brush Management. Out here there are also a few regional programs run through Sul Ross State University's Borderlands Research Institute (BRI) that most of the state does not have. This guide covers what each one paid when we checked, what it takes to apply, and which ones you should not count on this year. Our [individual plant treatment article](/articles/individual-plant-treatment/) covers what Practice 314 is. This one goes into the rates, the rules and the Trans-Pecos programs.

Every program below sets its own terms and changes them. Treat the numbers as a starting point for a phone call, not a promise of money.

## EQIP Practice 314: the FY2025 Texas rates

Texas NRCS publishes a payment schedule every fiscal year. About 80 Texas counties carry their own, higher rows for brush management. None of the Trans-Pecos counties do: not Jeff Davis, Brewster, Presidio, Pecos, Reeves, Culberson or Hudspeth. The statewide rates apply there.[^1] These are the **FY2025** statewide rates for the scenarios that matter most on rangeland:[^1]

| Practice 314 scenario (FY2025, Texas statewide)  | Regular, per acre | Historically underserved, per acre |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Individual plant treatment, 50 to 200 plants/ac  | $21.30            | $25.56                             |
| Individual plant treatment, 201 to 400 plants/ac | $51.61            | $61.93                             |
| Chemical control, aerial or ground application   | $35.72            | $42.86                             |
| Chemical control, broadcast application          | $75.46            | $90.55                             |
| Mechanical control, 11 to 30% canopy             | $128.57           | $154.29                            |
| Mechanical control, 31 to 50% canopy             | $203.66           | $244.39                            |
| Mechanical control, over 50% canopy              | $364.02           | $436.82                            |

These rates change every year. In FY2024 the two individual plant treatment tiers paid $21.85 and $53.16, a little more than in FY2025.[^2] Texas NRCS says each new schedule comes out in the first quarter of the fiscal year, October through December.[^3] We have not seen the FY2026 Texas schedule. Confirm current rates with NRCS before you plan around any number in this table.

## How EQIP pays

EQIP does not pay a share of your contractor's invoice. It pays a fixed rate per acre for the scenario NRCS assigns, and that rate is set from NRCS's own estimate of what the practice typically costs. Regular rates cover up to 75 percent of that estimate. Producers who count as historically underserved can get up to 90 percent; that group includes beginning, veteran, limited-resource and socially disadvantaged producers.[^4] In every Practice 314 row of the FY2025 Texas schedule, the underserved rate is exactly 1.2 times the regular rate, which is what you get from 90 percent against 75 percent of the same estimate.[^1]

Three things follow from that.

**NRCS picks the scenario, not you or your contractor.** The field office decides which line of the schedule your treatment matches. No NRCS document we found names drone or aerial spot application of individual plants. The closest fit on paper is the individual plant treatment scenario, but whether it applies to a given job is the field office's call. Ask before you assume.

**Approval comes before work.** Plan on having a signed contract or written approval in hand before any treatment goes on the ground, and ask your field office directly whether work started early can be paid at all. The same goes for every program below.

**Applications are ranked in batches.** For FY2026, NRCS set a national January 15, 2026 batching date for the first funding round, and it applied to Texas.[^5] The Texas EQIP page has listed November 13, 2026 as an application deadline. We could not confirm which ranking period that date closes, so ask your service center.[^6] Applications that come in after a batching date wait for the next one.

There is also a per-person payment limit in the regulations, and its status is uncertain. In November 2025 Agri-Pulse reported that the farm bill extension then in effect left out the payment-limit language for EQIP and CSP.[^7] We are not quoting a limit here. If your project is large, ask NRCS what applies now.

## Why plant density sets the tier

Look at the table again. The schedule pays individual plant treatment in two density bands, 50 to 200 plants per acre and 201 to 400. It has no individual-plant row below 50 or above 400, and mechanical work is paid by canopy cover.[^1] The difference between the bands is large: the higher one pays about two and a half times the lower.

That makes plant density the most important fact in your application, and it is often the one nobody has measured. A pasture someone guesses at 150 plants per acre may count out at 250 once every plant is mapped, or the other way around. That count determines which line NRCS can consider. It also shows where waiting leads. Brush that thickens past the individual-plant range lands in scenarios that pay more per acre because the work costs more per acre.[^1] Our [ballpark tool](/tools/ballpark/) gives a rough first look at your place before anyone flies it.

## Grass needs rest after treatment

Killing brush only pays off if grass comes back, and grass needs time without grazing to do it. AgriLife and BRI's Trans-Pecos range guide defines deferment as no grazing for one growing season and rest as no grazing for a year or more. It notes that grass plants recover through rest or deferment, and that after chemical treatment livestock are more likely to eat recently treated plants, so a deferment period should be applied in some cases.[^8] Hexazinone is also slow out here. Its label says that in low-rainfall areas of the West it may take up to two years to see the full effect, and that it needs soil moisture to activate.[^9]

NRCS pays for that part too, under Practice 528, Prescribed Grazing. The FY2025 Texas statewide rates were $9.18 per acre for the standard scenario, $11.94 for rest rotation grazing and $3.50 for range deferment.[^1] Ask your field office whether 528 can go in the same contract as the brush work. It is the piece that protects what you spent. Our article on [brush control and cattle profitability](/articles/brush-control-cattle-profitability/) covers what the grass is worth once it returns.

## CSP pays less per acre, with one wildlife exception

The Conservation Stewardship Program is a separate NRCS program, and it pays on a different basis. In the FY2025 Texas CSP schedule, individual plant treatment under Practice 314 was $2.91 per acre at 50 to 200 plants and $7.09 at 201 to 400, a fraction of the EQIP rates for the same scenarios.[^10] The same schedule lists enhancement E314A, "Brush management to improve wildlife habitat," at $17.22 per acre.[^10] If wildlife is your main reason for treating, ask about E314A. Our piece on [wildlife valuation and brush management](/articles/wildlife-valuation-brush-management/) covers the habitat side.

## Trans-Pecos programs through BRI

**Greater Big Bend Conservation Partnership.** BRI runs this with a $3.5 million commitment from NRCS under the Regional Conservation Partnership Program. When it was announced in April 2023, it was set to reimburse about 50 percent of project costs for landowners in Brewster, Jeff Davis and Presidio counties. Brush management, mechanical and chemical, was on the eligible list, and the application portal was open year-round.[^11] That announcement is more than three years old. Call BRI to confirm funds remain before you count on them.

**Partners for Habitat.** BRI runs this one with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. In 2023 it had $150,000 available and covered Brewster, Terrell, Pecos, Reeves, Culberson, Hudspeth, Jeff Davis and Presidio counties, again with mechanical and chemical brush management eligible.[^11]

**Trans-Pecos Grasslands and Riparian Conservation Initiative.** BRI lists more than $2 million in federal funds to cost-share work under this initiative, including chemical and mechanical brush management. The same page lists the Alamito Creek Conservation Initiative in Presidio County and the Trans-Pecos Grazing Lands Conservation Partnership.[^12]

## State programs: what applies and what does not

**TPWD Landowner Incentive Program (LIP).** LIP contributes 50 to 75 percent of total project cost, and selective brush management is one of its practices.[^13] That fits plant-by-plant work: TPWD's own West Texas brush guidance says individual plant treatments provide "the ultimate in selectivity."[^14] But TPWD's LIP page currently reads "2026 – no request for proposals."[^13] Watch for the next round; there is nothing to apply for right now.

**TSSWCB Water Supply Enhancement Program (WSEP).** WSEP cost-shares brush control up to 70 percent, but only inside its project watersheds. None of the 23 is in the Trans-Pecos.[^15] Don't count on it here.

**Texas A&M Forest Service wildfire grants.** The Forest Service's prescribed fire grants are statewide and paid $22.50 to $30 per acre in the 2026 round. Its mechanical fuel reduction grants cover 39 Central Texas counties only.[^16] If you plan to follow brush treatment with prescribed fire once grass carries a burn, the fire grant may apply. The mechanical grant will not.

## How to apply

1. **Know your density first.** Get a plant count, or at least a solid estimate, for each pasture you want treated. It drives the scenario conversation.
2. **Call your local NRCS service center.** Ask which Practice 314 scenario your brush and your treatment method would fall under, what the current fiscal year's rate is, and which batching date you are aiming at. Ask about pairing 528 Prescribed Grazing.
3. **Call BRI** if you are in Brewster, Jeff Davis or Presidio county, or anywhere in its Partners for Habitat footprint. Ask what funds are open now.
4. **Sequence the work.** Approval first, then treatment, then deferment while the grass recovers, then a check on what died. Don't let a contractor start before the paperwork is signed.
5. **Keep records.** Maps of what was treated, dates, rainfall and before-and-after photos make it easier to certify the practice and to plan follow-up.

## Why a plant count helps

A plant-by-plant survey does two jobs in a cost-share application. Before treatment, it gives you and the field office a measured density for each pasture, so the scenario discussion starts from data rather than a guess. After treatment, a second survey shows which plants died and which did not. That record documents the work was done, and with hexazinone's slow response out here,[^9] it shows what needs follow-up. Our [brush return calculator](/tools/brush-roi/) has a cost-share panel for working through the per-acre rates against your own numbers, and our [calculator scenarios](/articles/brush-return-calculator-scenarios/) walk through worked examples. If you are not sure this kind of work fits your place, start with [who brush work is for](/articles/who-brush-work-is-for/).

Cost-share money is real out here, but it runs on NRCS's schedule, not yours, so call before you plan the job.
