A lot of people who buy land out here hear about the "wildlife exemption" before they hear about anything else. It is not an exemption, and getting that straight is the first step to keeping it. This article covers what wildlife-use valuation is, where brush management fits in it, how much brush work the Trans-Pecos standard calls for each year, and what Texas Parks and Wildlife asks you to leave alone while you do it.

One thing up front: we are not tax advisers. Your county appraisal district decides whether your land qualifies, and a TPWD biologist can help you write a plan. Nothing here replaces either one.

## What wildlife-use valuation is

Texas lets land that already has agricultural valuation, the kind people call 1-d-1 or ag valuation, change its qualifying use from grazing or crops to wildlife management and keep that valuation.[^1] The legal basis is Texas Tax Code 23.51(7).[^1] TPWD puts it plainly: for property tax purposes, wildlife management is agriculture, there is no change in the valuation, only a change in the qualifying practice, and "there is no wildlife 'exemption'."[^2]

That matters for two reasons. First, if your land does not already have ag valuation, wildlife management does not create it. Second, because it is a valuation and not an exemption, it depends on what you actually do on the ground each year. The land has to be actively managed to sustain a breeding, migrating or wintering population of native wildlife.[^1]

## The seven practices and the three-of-seven rule

The Tax Code lists seven wildlife management practices: habitat control, erosion control, predator control, providing supplemental water, providing supplemental food, providing shelters, and making census counts.[^1] You pick which three of the seven to carry out each year.[^1]

The plan goes on TPWD form PWD 885-W7000 and is filed with your county central appraisal district. It must address a separate practice in at least three of the seven categories.[^2] TPWD's regional guidelines set how much of each practice counts, what the rules call the degree of intensity.[^1] TPWD does not have to be involved in your plan, but its private-lands biologists will help write one on request.[^2][^3]

Brush management sits under the first practice, habitat control.[^4]

## How much brush work the Trans-Pecos standard calls for

The Trans-Pecos guidelines define brush management as the removal or establishment of woody plants, including "the selective removal or suppression of target woody species" to allow more production of desirable trees, shrubs, grasses and forbs.[^4] The intensity standard reads:

> Brush Management practices should annually affect a minimum of 10% of the total area designated in the plan, or a minimum of 100 acres annually, whichever is smaller.[^4]

The math is simple:

- **640-acre plan area.** Ten percent is 64 acres. That is smaller than 100, so 64 acres a year meets the standard.[^4]
- **1,000-acre plan area.** Ten percent is 100 acres, the same as the cap, so 100 acres a year.[^4]
- **5,000-acre plan area.** Ten percent is 500 acres, which is more than 100, so 100 acres a year meets the standard.[^4]

Note the words "area designated in the plan." That is the acreage in your wildlife plan, which is not always every acre you own. Our [brush ROI tool](/tools/brush-roi/) works out the yearly figure from your plan acreage, and the [ballpark tool](/tools/ballpark/) gives a rough idea of the work on your place.

Brush management is one habitat-control option among several. The same appendix lists prescribed burning, range enhancement, exotic plant control such as saltcedar, riparian management, fence changes for pronghorn and bighorn, and planting native woody plants, each with its own standard.[^4] Brush work is not required. It is a good fit where brush is already a problem you want to deal with.

## What TPWD asks you to keep

The standard is not just an acreage number. The same paragraph says the practice "includes retaining the proper kind, amount, and distribution of woody cover for selected species."[^4] Planning has to consider wildlife cover needs, soil types, slope angle and direction, erosion, and how you will control the brush coming back.[^4] It also includes keeping snags, the standing dead trees cavity nesters use, and it requires that herbicides be "applied in strict accordance with label directions."[^4]

TPWD's broader brush guidance for West Texas makes the same point from the other side: "inadequate cover can be just as detrimental as too much brush."[^5] The right amount depends on the animals you are managing for. TPWD's optimum woody cover runs 15 to 40 percent for mule deer, 10 to 35 percent for scaled quail and 1 to 10 percent for pronghorn.[^5] For mule deer and scaled quail together it suggests about 25 to 30 percent.[^6] We go into how to pick a number in [how much brush to keep](/articles/how-much-brush-to-keep/).

The siting rules are just as specific. Keep woody buffers at least 200 feet wide on each side of draws. Leave ridges, upper slopes and canyon headers alone. Keep a few mature mesquite.[^5] TPWD also warns that herbicides can suppress some grasses and especially forbs for one to two years in treated areas, which affects deer and quail in the short term, and that a kill sometimes takes two growing seasons.[^5]

So a habitat-control year that knocks down 64 acres of everything does not match the guidelines. A year that takes out the right plants and leaves the right ones does.

## Where individual plant treatment fits

TPWD's own language points toward selectivity. Its West Texas brush appendix says ground methods let a manager treat specific undesirable species while avoiding more desirable woody plants, and that "individual plant treatments provide the ultimate in selectivity."[^5] The introduction to the Trans-Pecos guidelines lists extent, pattern, selection and method as the principles of a brush program, says "Individual plant treatment may be all you need, depending on the amount of brush present," and says removal of desirable plants should be kept to a minimum.[^2]

That is the idea behind [individual plant treatment](/articles/individual-plant-treatment/): you choose each plant that goes and each one that stays. Our work follows the same pattern. We fly a drone survey that maps brush plant by plant, mark targets and keepers against your plan's cover goal and buffers, treat the targets with hexazinone pellets, and fly again later to see what died and what came back. TPWD does not endorse drones or any vendor, and there are no published results yet for drone-applied pellets. What the guidelines describe is the selectivity, and that does not depend on how the pellet gets to the plant.

Pellets have limits worth knowing. The hexazinone (Velpar L CU) label warns that desirable trees can absorb it through roots that extend into treated areas, and it carries a federal groundwater advisory.[^7] Keeping an oak means keeping pellets far enough from it, not hoping. The label also notes that results can take up to two years in the low-rainfall West.[^7] In a broadcast herbicide project on about 4,000 acres in West Texas, shrubland birds declined but grassland birds did not increase, and the researchers suggested mechanical removal of the standing snags left behind may be necessary.[^8] Herbicide work leaves dead trees standing. Plan for that rather than expecting open grassland the next spring.

## Timing and the canyons

If your country includes black-capped vireo habitat, timing matters. In the Trans-Pecos the vireo uses canyon bottoms and slopes in the eastern part of the region and, to a limited extent, the Chisos Mountains.[^9] TPWD's guidance is to do brush work there only in the non-breeding season, September through February, and not to apply herbicides during the breeding season except basal treatments or individual treatment of pricklypear pads.[^9] The vireo was taken off the federal endangered list in 2018,[^12] but TPWD's Trans-Pecos guidance still carries these recommendations.[^9]

The same guidance warns against large one-time treatments. Poorly planned brush work "may remove too much low growing woody cover, especially when large acreages are treated at one time," and big, sudden changes in shrub canopy from one year to the next should be avoided.[^9] A steady 64 or 100 acres a year, with draws buffered and canyons left for the right months, fits that better than one big job.

## Why it is a yearly job, and why records matter

The standard says "annually." Brush management only counts as one of your three practices in the years you do it. Over ten years, 64 acres a year works through the whole of a 640-acre plan area, and by the time you come back around, the first acres are ready for a look at regrowth. TPWD's planning language expects that, since your plan has to account for controlling re-invasion.[^4]

Because the appraisal district decides whether you qualify, you want to be able to show what you did. Keep a record of which acres were treated, which species, what method and product, and on what dates. Ask your appraisal district what it wants to see each year. A map of treated plants and retained cover, made from the same survey that guided the work, is one way to have that on hand, and a repeat flight shows whether the treatment took.

## Who this matters most for

Texas land is changing hands. Texas A&M's Natural Resources Institute counts more than 1,000 acres of working land lost a day and about 56 million acres facing transfer between generations.[^10] A third of Texas landowners are absentee, and 32 percent have less than ten years of experience on the land.[^10] Researchers on the Edwards Plateau noted that land there is being bought by people who do not depend on land income and may be more tolerant of brush.[^11]

If that describes you, wildlife valuation is often what first puts brush on your list. You may not have a crew or equipment, and you may not be on the place often. That is the situation we think about in [who brush work is for](/articles/who-brush-work-is-for/). If you are weighing how to pay for it, [paying for brush work in the Trans-Pecos](/articles/paying-for-brush-work-trans-pecos/) covers cost-share programs, and [why brush control](/articles/why-brush-control/) covers what brush does to your grass and wildlife in the first place.

Talk to your appraisal district and a TPWD biologist first. Then decide whether brush management is one of your three.
