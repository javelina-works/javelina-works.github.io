Most brush questions start with what to kill. The better question on a lot of Trans-Pecos country is what to keep. Texas Parks and Wildlife puts it plainly in its West Texas brush guidance: managers should avoid excessive removal of woody cover because "inadequate cover can be just as detrimental as too much brush."[^1] This article covers TPWD's cover targets, what staying inside them costs you in grass, and how to lay out a treatment so the brush that stays is the brush doing a job. If you want the broader case for brush work first, start with [why brush control](/articles/why-brush-control/).

## Brush is habitat until it isn't

TPWD lists what woody plants give wildlife: escape cover, loafing cover, nesting habitat, thermal protection, and food in the form of browse and mast.[^1] The same document lists what too much brush does: it hinders movement, cuts visibility of approaching predators, competes with grass and forbs for moisture and nutrients, and can favor more predators.[^1] Both lists are true on the same ranch. The goal is a number in between, and which number depends on which animals you are managing for.

## TPWD's cover targets by species

TPWD's Trans-Pecos guidelines give an optimum range of woody cover for long-term production and survival of each species:[^1]

| Species           | Optimum woody cover |
| ----------------- | ------------------- |
| Pronghorn         | 1–10%               |
| Bobwhite quail    | 5–25%               |
| Scaled quail      | 10–35%              |
| Mule deer         | 15–40%              |
| Turkey            | 40–60%              |
| White-tailed deer | 50–75%              |

A few notes from the source. The pronghorn figure counts yucca and cholla. The scaled quail figure counts very small shrubs. The turkey figure should preferably be trees and mast producers.[^1] TPWD also says woody cover is only one of many habitat parameters, so hitting a number does not make a pasture good habitat by itself.[^1]

The scaled quail appendix narrows it further. It calls 10 to 20 percent brush suitable if the cover is the right kind, meaning low-growing. Where a ranch is managed for both mule deer and scaled quail, it recommends keeping brush canopy at about 25 to 30 percent.[^2]

## Thicker brush trades mule deer for whitetail

The mule deer appendix makes the shift explicit. Research shows mule deer prefer brush canopy of 40 percent or less, while white-tailed deer respond well above 50 percent. Where brush is getting taller and denser, TPWD says, the habitat is becoming better for whitetail and worse for mule deer.[^3] Among its recommended practices for mule deer country is controlling invading cedar and mesquite where densities go beyond what mule deer need.[^3] The same appendix lists mesquite and juniper as mule deer browse, which is one more reason the target is a range and not zero.[^3]

## There is no single right number

Look at the table again. Pronghorn want almost no brush, and whitetail want most of the country in it. No one figure serves both. TPWD's own planning guidance says a good brush program comes down to extent, pattern, selection and method, and gives the example that if your goals include white-tailed deer, you may only want to clear about half. It adds that individual plant treatment may be all you need, depending on how much brush is there.[^4]

So the first step is not a treatment plan. It is deciding what the ranch is for. A pronghorn and grassland pasture on the flats, a mule deer and quail pasture in the foothills, and a whitetail draw can all sit on one place, each with its own target. Write them down by pasture before anyone talks about acres.

## What staying in the band costs in grass

Keeping more brush means growing less grass, and it helps to see how much. We ran a worked example for the [ROI calculator](/tools/brush-roi/): a 500-acre Igneous Hill and Mountain, Mixed Prairie pasture in the Davis Mountains, with juniper at 35 percent canopy. The site's NRCS reference production in Jeff Davis County is about 2,048 pounds per acre in a normal year.[^5] We applied a published redberry juniper forage curve from west-central Texas (Johnson et al. 1999)[^6] and counted only part of the ground as grazable, since it is steep and rocky.

Cutting juniper to 10 percent brings back about 700 pounds of grass an acre in a normal year, the most the Trans-Pecos research supports. Stopping at 20 percent brings back about 524.[^5][^6] Stopping at 20 instead of 10 gives up about a quarter of the grass, and about a sixth of the 20-year grazing value. In exchange, the pasture stays inside the mule deer band and closer to the 25 to 30 percent TPWD suggests for mule deer with scaled quail.[^1][^2] That is a model, not a measurement on your ground, but it puts a size on the choice. The calculator's wildlife panel shows the same trade-off for your own numbers: it lists which species' cover bands the brush you keep falls inside.

## Where to treat and where to leave brush

TPWD's West Texas guidance is specific about layout, and most of it is about what to leave.

**Leave the draws.** Keep a buffer of woody plants along each creek or draw, at least 200 feet on each side. TPWD says that stringer of trees and shrubs prevents erosion, serves as a travel corridor and gives screening cover. In very narrow drainages, the best option may be to leave all of it.[^1]

**Leave ridges, upper slopes and canyon heads.** Focus treatment on the deep-soiled flats and lowlands. TPWD says the forage response on lower slopes and flats will be 4 to 5 times greater, or more, than on rocky, shallow-soiled ground.[^1] The ground that grows the most grass after treatment is usually not the ground wildlife lean on for cover.

**Keep some mature mesquite.** TPWD recommends leaving a few big mesquite trees for shade, for the soil, since mesquite is a nitrogen-fixing legume, and for the bean crop, which it calls excellent forage.[^1]

**Small patches beat big blocks.** The scaled quail appendix says mosaic patterns give more edge and help quail more than strips, and that small patches or strips of brush beat large blocks of brush and large openings.[^2] TPWD's endangered species appendix warns that poorly planned work can remove too much low cover, especially when large acreages are treated at one time, and that hurts deer, quail, small mammals and songbirds as well as vireos.[^7] Keep travel corridors that link bedding and feeding areas.[^4]

**Keep snags.** TPWD's habitat control standard for wildlife valuation includes retaining snags,[^8] and the West Texas guidance notes that the standing skeletons herbicide leaves behind can be an important habitat component for some species.[^1]

**Spare the broadleaf shrubs.** The TPWD and USFWS black-capped vireo guidelines recommend selective removal of juniper, mesquite and pricklypear to hold the right shrub canopy, avoiding radical canopy changes over large areas from one year to the next, and choosing individual plant methods where practical to avoid damaging live oak, shin oak, Texas oak, hackberry, Texas persimmon, sumac, redbud and elm.[^7] That list is written for vireo country, but it is a good default anywhere you want variety in the woody cover.

## Davis Mountains species to plan around

**Montezuma quail.** TPWD describes them in the high desert grasslands of the Davis Mountains and other Trans-Pecos ranges. They prefer grassier areas than scaled quail, nest in dense grassy cover, and depend on underground tubers and bulbs for food and water.[^9] A Texas A&M quail researcher, speaking on Marfa Public Radio, said the birds need grass at least 4 inches tall plus woody cover from oaks, junipers or piñon.[^10] Grass and some trees together are the point. Thinning juniper can help the grass. Taking all of it does not help the quail.

**Mexican spotted owl.** In Texas it occurs only in the Davis and Guadalupe Mountains, and it has been federally threatened since 1993.[^11] If you have owl habitat, talk to TPWD or USFWS before planning work there.

**Hinckley oak.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service lists Hinckley oak as federally threatened, since 1988.[^12] We have not confirmed its county range against the federal listing, so treat any small, scrubby oak on rocky ground as something to identify before you plan around it.

**Black-capped vireo.** The vireo came off the federal endangered list in 2018.[^13] TPWD's Trans-Pecos guidance still covers it: in the eastern Trans-Pecos and on igneous soils in the Chisos, it uses canyon bottoms and slopes with sandpaper oak, Vasey oak, Texas persimmon and similar shrubs. TPWD says brush work in vireo habitat should happen only in the non-breeding season, September through February.[^7] That window is a sensible default for any canyon work.

## Don't expect fast wildlife results

Wildlife response is slower and less certain than the tables make it look. In West Texas, a partnership treated about 4,000 acres with a broadcast herbicide aimed at mesquite. The Borderlands Research Institute reported that many shrubland bird species declined after treatment, but grassland birds did not increase. The researchers suggested mechanical removal of snags may be necessary.[^14] That cuts against the snag advice above, and it is an honest tension: dead standing brush helps some species and may keep open-country birds away.

Herbicide also has a short-term cost. TPWD notes that grasses and especially forbs can be suppressed for one to two years in treated areas, which matters to deer and quail, and that a kill sometimes takes two growing seasons.[^1] The hexazinone label says that in low-rainfall areas of the West, the full effect can take up to two years, and moisture is needed to activate it in the soil.[^15] Plan for years, not a season.

## Keeping oaks next to hexazinone

Hexazinone pellets go on the soil and rain carries them to the roots. The hexazinone (Velpar L CU) label warns that care must be taken near desirable trees or shrubs because they "can absorb [it] through roots extending into treated areas."[^15] Roots run well past a tree's canopy. So keeping an oak means keeping a setback from it, not just skipping the oak itself. A cedar growing up through a live oak may have to be left or handled some other way. The label also carries a groundwater advisory and prohibits applying where surface water is present.[^15] That is one more reason to leave the draws alone. The [individual plant treatment article](/articles/individual-plant-treatment/) covers the label setbacks in more detail.

## How a plant-by-plant map lets you choose

Every recommendation above needs to know where each plant is. You cannot hold a pasture at 25 percent, keep 200 feet along every draw, save the mature mesquite and spare the oaks unless you can see them first. TPWD calls individual plant treatments "the ultimate in selectivity."[^1] That is TPWD describing the method, not endorsing any company.

Our survey flies the ranch and maps every woody plant by species. From that map you can mark the draw buffers, ridges and canyon heads to leave, the oaks and big mesquite to keep, and the treatment setbacks around them, then see what canopy is left in each pasture before anything is treated. If brush management is one of your wildlife valuation practices, TPWD's Trans-Pecos standard is at least 10 percent of the plan area or 100 acres a year, whichever is smaller, and it includes "retaining the proper kind, amount, and distribution of woody cover." [^8] TPWD is clear that this is a change in the qualifying agricultural use, not an exemption, and the plan is filed with your county appraisal district.[^4] The [wildlife valuation article](/articles/wildlife-valuation-brush-management/) goes into that.
