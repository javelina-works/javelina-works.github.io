This is the long version of a question we hear on nearly every ranch: is the brush actually costing me anything? [The short version](/articles/why-manage-brush/) answers it in a few paragraphs. This one shows the work: what range scientists in Texas have measured over the last sixty years on mesquite and on cedar, which out here means redberry and Ashe juniper. Where the research is mixed, it says so. Where brush is doing a job for you, it says that too.

## How the brush got here

Early accounts of the Trans-Pecos describe open grassland with scattered shrubs. Waist-high grass was reported along Terlingua Creek and in Tornillo Flats, where eroded desert sits today, and mesquite grew only as scattered plants in small isolated stands.[^1] A Texas Parks and Wildlife review puts the region's fastest vegetation change in the last 120 years and names the drivers: livestock grazing, the suppression of fire, and frequent drought working together.[^1]

Grazing did two things. In the 1880s and 1890s, ranches here ran 4 to 12 acres per animal unit, against present recommendations of 75 to 200 or more.[^1] Heavy grazing stripped the fine fuel that carried grass fires, and fire was what had held mesquite in check and kept juniper pinned to rocky outcrops and north slopes.[^2][^3] Cattle also moved mesquite, eating the pods and passing the seed in fresh fertilizer far from the parent tree.[^2] At Texas A&M's La Copita station in south Texas, mesquite seedlings came up and survived at high rates even in a drought year, and thinning the grass made no difference, which led the authors to conclude that seed dispersal by livestock, not rainfall or grass competition, now decides where mesquite spreads.[^4]

Two more factors matter. In a USDA study at Temple, mesquite seedling survival in grassland rose from 1.5 percent at pre-industrial CO2 levels to 15 percent at modern levels and 28 percent at projected future levels, because grasses pull less water from the soil when CO2 is higher.[^5] Wet cycles speed it up. On a north Texas site, mesquite canopy rose from 14.6 percent to 58.7 percent in twenty years, and the rate more than doubled during five years of rainfall 25 percent above normal.[^6] Redberry juniper spreads from established stands into the grassland beside them, carried by small mammals, wind, water and livestock.[^7]

## How much country it covers

Ashe juniper occupies about 8.6 million acres of Texas, much of it former grassland.[^10] Redberry juniper covers roughly 11.7 million acres.[^8] In 65 northwest Texas counties it went from about 6.3 million acres in 1948 to 10.1 million in 1982, roughly a 60 percent increase.[^7][^11] The federal rangeland inventory finds mesquite out of place on 17.2 percent of Texas non-federal rangeland and covering at least 30 percent of the ground on 7.5 percent of it; juniper covers at least 30 percent of the ground on 3.7 percent.[^12] About 60 percent of Texas is rangeland, so those are tens of millions of acres.[^13]

## What brush does to water

The honest answer has two halves.

A mature Ashe juniper canopy intercepts about 37 percent of the rain that falls on it, and redberry about 26 percent; the litter under the trees catches another 40 percent or so. Only about 20 percent of annual rainfall reaches mineral soil under Ashe juniper and 34 percent under redberry, compared with 82 percent under bunchgrass.[^14] An adult mesquite in north Texas can use up to 20 gallons of water a day in midsummer.[^2] On the North Concho near San Angelo, a treated mesquite pasture ran about 25 percent lower evapotranspiration than an untreated one at the peak of the growing season, about 1.8 inches less water over four years.[^15]

The second half is whether that saved water ever becomes a spring, a tank or a creek. Bradford Wilcox's review sets the conditions. There is little potential to increase streamflow where annual rainfall is under about 500 millimeters, which is about 20 inches. Above that line, look for existing springs or seeps fed by shallow soil over fractured rock. That is where saved water can get past the root zone instead of being used by whatever grows back.[^16] Rainfall in Texas drops to 8 inches near El Paso, and the state's own assessment judged that regions under 17 inches are unlikely to justify brush control for water yield at all.[^13] The healthy grassland savannas that remain in Jeff Davis, Presidio, Brewster and Hudspeth counties sit at the higher, wetter elevations.[^1]

A Texas Water Resources Institute review concluded that clearing dense juniper from uplands over permeable rock can raise spring and stream flow, that clearing ground with no subsurface connection to a stream will do little, and that regrowth has to be controlled or the gain fades.[^17] At Honey Creek on the Edwards Plateau, ten years of USGS monitoring found less evapotranspiration after cedar removal but no change in the relationship between rainfall and streamflow.[^18] The North Concho program cleared mesquite and cedar from roughly 1,200 of 3,100 square kilometers between 2000 and 2005 at a cost of about 14 million dollars and produced no perceptible increase in river flow, though the same hydrologists note that small Edwards Plateau springs and seeps do respond to clearing at pasture scale.[^19]

The fair summary for West Texas: brush control reliably cuts the water that trees use. Whether you see that water again depends on your rainfall, your rock, and whether you have a spring to feed. If someone promises water from brush control on 12 inches of rain, be skeptical.

## Forage and carrying capacity

The forage case is stronger and more consistent than the water case.

For mesquite, the relationship is a threshold. Ansley's team at Vernon found that warm-season midgrasses lose most of their production as canopy climbs past about 30 percent, and recommended keeping cover below it.[^20] In an economic analysis for a north Texas ranch, taking mesquite from 30 percent cover to 10 percent moved carrying capacity from about 30 acres per animal unit to about 14.[^21] McDaniel's team measured a 7 to 16 percent gain in grazing capacity over four years after aerial spraying, but grubbing, which tore up the soil, produced no gain at all.[^22]

The response is not automatic. Scifres and Polk sprayed a light infestation and got more than 95 percent stand reduction and about 3 kilograms more grass per hectare for every centimeter of rain.[^23] A later study from the same lab found no difference in herbaceous production between sprayed and untreated plots after six and seven growing seasons.[^24] Rain, soil, and what you do with the grass afterward decide the result.[^2]

For cedar the curve is steeper. Near Roscoe, forage fell from 1,148 pounds per acre with no juniper to 14 pounds under full canopy, with the loss accelerating until canopy reached about 34 percent.[^25] At San Angelo, 117 junipers per acre cut forage about 40 percent, and a closed canopy cut it about 85 percent.[^3] A Texas Tech analysis of redberry juniper control in the Rolling Plains put the internal rate of return at 27 percent with an eight-year payback, which beat buying more land.[^25] Ueckert's review was less rosy: only two of five economic analyses found juniper control feasible, and the ones that worked counted wildlife and recreation income alongside livestock.[^26]

## Wildlife: some of the brush is the point

A survey of 1,058 landowners across 48 Texas counties found forage and water were the top two reasons people wanted less brush.[^27] Wildlife is usually the reason they want to keep some.

Mule deer prefer brush canopy of 40 percent or less, and white-tailed deer do better above 50 percent, so taller, denser brush shifts Trans-Pecos country from mule deer habitat toward whitetail habitat.[^28] Extension guidance for deer, quail and turkey together is roughly 40 to 60 percent of the country in brush, with openings no more than 200 yards wide.[^29] Quail need a minimum of about 15 percent well-distributed brush and seldom forage more than 50 yards from woody cover, so wide clean clearings are the worst pattern for them.[^29][^30] In the Trans-Pecos the limiting factor for quail is not brush but nesting cover: bunchgrass to hide a nest in.[^31] That argues for opening brush that is choking out grass, not leaving it.

Golden-cheeked warblers are the hard case, though mostly a Hill Country one. Their whole nesting range is 33 counties of central Texas, in mature Ashe juniper mixed with hardwoods, trees at least 15 feet tall with trunks about five inches through and bark old enough to shred.[^32] The guidance there is to leave closed canopies on steep slopes and canyons alone and take only young juniper under 10 feet in the open.[^33] The principle travels: leave old trees on the rough ground and drainages, and work the flats.

## Cedar and wildfire

The Rock House fire was first reported on April 9, 2011 and burned 314,444 acres around Fort Davis before it was 95 percent contained on May 6.[^34] Juniper did not start it, but it changes how fire behaves.

Grass fire kills small cedar: 99 percent of Ashe juniper under about 6 feet tall died in burns carrying 1,500 kilograms per hectare or more of fine fuel.[^9] Big trees are hard to kill with a surface fire, and once trees stand within about 26 feet of each other a fire can move crown to crown.[^10] Redberry juniper canopies ignite readily when foliage moisture drops below 70 percent, which a Rolling Plains study saw from late January on.[^35] About 97 percent of older redberry plants resprout from the base after fire, so one burn does not finish them, and a fire every 20 to 30 years is thought to be what it takes to keep them out of a grassland.[^11] A cedar-choked slope is a fuel ladder next to your house. Thinning it to a savanna spacing is a fire question as much as a forage question.

## Soil

Brush and bare ground go together. As juniper canopy closes, the grass between trees thins, the soil bares, and runoff concentrates into rills. A USDA erosion-model guide for Edwards Plateau sites puts soil loss at about 0.9 tons per acre per year under tall grass with under 5 percent juniper, 3.9 tons under overgrazed grass, and 5.2 tons once juniper passes 30 percent canopy with the understory gone, and it treats that last state as one that cannot be reversed within a management lifetime.[^36] Davenport and colleagues framed the general rule: on sites with high erosion potential, a small drop in ground cover can tip erosion from slow to rapid.[^37] In the Trans-Pecos, bare ground sheds water, the soil caps over, and runoff in place of infiltration is the main reason springs and creeks in old survey records went dry.[^1]

## Land value

Nobody has a clean study showing clearing brush adds a set dollar amount per acre, and the economists say so. Teague's team left land value out of their mesquite analysis because land values are often unrelated to productivity and are driven by speculative buyers.[^21] An Edwards Plateau policy review found land already cost more than livestock and hunting could return, and warned that owners planning to sell within a decade might avoid brush work because buyers like a wooded view.[^38]

A Junction appraiser's view of the market: ranches cleared wall to wall in the 1940s through 1960s command the lowest prices today. Heavily wooded ranches with wildlife cover sell higher but are discounted for the cost of bringing them into shape. The premium goes to places where the low ground is opened for grass and the surrounding cover is left, and live water keeps demand strong.[^39] That matches the forage and wildlife research: sculpted brush, not cleared brush, is the goal.

## When leaving it alone is the right answer

Sometimes the research says do nothing.

Leave it when the rainfall is not there to pay back a water goal. Under roughly 17 to 20 inches, treat for grass and wildlife or not at all.[^13][^16] Leave mature cedar on steep slopes, canyons and drainages, where it holds soil and makes habitat.[^33][^36] Leave enough cover for the deer and quail you want, in strips and mottes rather than isolated clumps that become traps for a covey.[^29][^30] Leave old juniper if you are in warbler country.[^32] And leave it if you cannot follow up. Forage gains fade as brush returns and juniper seedlings come back by the hundreds per acre, so a treatment you cannot maintain buys a few good years.[^26][^24]
