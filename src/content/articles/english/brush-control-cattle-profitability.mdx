Brush control is a capital expense. It either pays itself back or it does not. The research on whether it pays is older and larger than most people expect, most of it done in Texas over forty years. This is a plain reading of that work for a cow-calf operation in the Trans-Pecos and the Davis Mountains. Rainfall changes the answer, so we say where each study was done. Throughout, "cedar" means what it means around here: redberry and Ashe juniper.

When 1,058 Texas landowners in 48 counties were asked why they manage brush, the two main reasons were to increase forage and to conserve water.[^1] The [companion article](/articles/why-manage-brush/) covers what brush costs. This one covers what control returns.

## What the grass does when the brush comes out

The relationship between brush and grass is not a straight line. McGinty's summary of the Edwards Plateau juniper work: a low stony hill site with 117 mature redberry junipers per acre produced about 1,156 pounds of forage per acre against a potential of 1,909 with no juniper, a 40 percent loss. A closed canopy takes the same site to roughly 283 pounds, an 85 percent loss, and the carrying capacity from one animal unit on 20 acres to one on 135.[^2] Mesquite behaves the same way. In a north Texas savanna, the warm-season midgrasses that carry a cow herd were the most sensitive, with losses steepening above about 30 percent mesquite cover.[^3]

Taking the brush out reverses some of that, but not all at once. In north-central Texas, aerial herbicide on light and heavy mesquite stands raised grazing capacity 7 and 16 percent over the following four years.[^4] A 2021 Rolling Plains study measured perennial grass 73 percent higher between dead mesquite and 55 percent higher under them in the first year. In the second year the grass faded and annual forbs took over, which the authors tie to a fall rainfall more than double normal.[^5]

The brush does not stand still if you leave it. On a clay loam site near Vernon, mesquite canopy cover in an untreated pasture went from 14.6 percent to 58.7 percent in twenty years, about 2.2 percentage points a year. A pasture root-plowed to zero in 1974 was back to 21.9 percent by 1995.[^6]

## From pounds of grass to pounds of calf

Economists turn forage into money through carrying capacity. In the Teague, Ansley, Kreuter, McGrann and Pinchak analysis of mesquite on the Waggoner Ranch in north Texas, clay-loam pasture at 30 percent mesquite cover carried one animal unit on 30 acres. With cover brought down to 10 percent, the same land carried one on 14.[^7] The grazing capacity estimates built for the North Concho brush program above San Angelo say the same for drier country: heavy mesquite at 32 acres per animal unit with no control, 24.3 with initial control, follow-up and deferment; heavy cedar from 45.1 acres down to 28.6.[^8]

On animal performance the evidence is thinner and more honest than the sales literature. The one ranch-scale cow-calf study that tracked conception rate, weaned calf percentage, weaning weight and pounds of calf per cow across mesquite restoration strategies for seven years found the untreated, continuously grazed herd did better in the first two years, and differences were minimal over the last five.[^9] Treatment did not make heavier calves. What it did was hold capacity up while the untreated country slid.

In the Trans-Pecos the scale is different. Texas Parks and Wildlife puts present-day stocking recommendations for the region at 75 to 200 or more acres per animal unit.[^10] A 20 percent change on that base is a few cows, not a herd, and the dollars have to be run for your own place.

## Does it pay? What the economists found

Whitson and Scifres ran the first serious numbers for mesquite in 1981. Over 20 years on deep Rolling Plains sites, aerial herbicide returned 9.6 to 17.9 percent a year across the cattle prices they tested, $37 to $47.50 per hundredweight in 1978 dollars. Chaining returned 7.1 to 12.5 percent. Herbicide returns were the more sensitive to price.[^11]

Teague and colleagues' 2001 sensitivity analysis is the one most often cited. For a hypothetical 20,000-acre north Texas ranch over 30 years at a 5 percent discount rate, a root-killing aerial herbicide at $23 an acre (2001 dollars) with a 20-year life produced a net present value of $12.20 an acre and a benefit-cost ratio of 2.3. Prescribed fire at $2.50 an acre every five to seven years, even assuming it was only half as effective at reducing brush, produced $17.30 an acre and a ratio of 6.8. The herbicide would have had to cost under $12 an acre to compete.[^7]

The Edwards Plateau work is less encouraging. Lee, Conner, Mjelde, Richardson and Stuth simulated representative ranches in four regions of the Plateau and found net present value fell with brush control in three of the four. Cost shares of 7 to 31 percent of treatment cost were needed just to break even.[^12] Hamilton and Conner, reviewing three decades of these analyses in 2004, concluded that "in many instances, it is no longer economically efficient based solely on revenues from livestock production to implement brush management practices without a cost-share source."[^13]

## What moves the payback

Hamilton and Conner's worked example shows the levers. One thousand acres of 60 percent Ashe juniper canopy, two-way chained at $20 an acre and burned in years two and eight at $5 and $4 (2004 dollars), with a 50 percent cost share and an 8 percent discount rate, broke even in year nine and returned 9.87 percent. Chaining alone broke even a year sooner but returned less over twelve years, because capacity peaked 87.5 percent above the start in year five and slid back toward 43 percent as the cedar returned. With the burns it reached 119 percent in year seven and held.[^13]

So the payback moves with four things. Treatment cost per acre: Ansley, Teague and Pinchak costed five pasture burns on the Waggoner at an average of $2.57 an acre in 1996, with the dozer lines the biggest item.[^14] Brush density, because dense stands cost more per acre to treat than thin ones.[^13] Cattle prices and the going rate for grass; Teague's analysis used $90 per animal unit per year.[^7] And rainfall, which decides how fast the grass comes and whether a burn can happen at all.[^7]

## Why timing matters more than method

Every Texas A&M author on this subject says the same thing. McGinty and Ueckert: "The effective treatment life of expensive reclamation treatments is usually not sufficient to recover treatment costs."[^15] Noble Research Institute's Steven Smith: "Prevention is more efficient, more successful and less expensive than controlling mature brush encroachment after the fact."[^16]

The reason is arithmetic. At low to moderate densities, treating individual plants is often the cheaper and more selective option; dense stands force broadcast methods.[^15] Teague's group adds that an effective burn is more certain when mesquite cover is under 20 percent, and that past 20 percent "the use of the more expensive herbicide treatment may be unavoidable."[^7]

Maintenance is the other half. Every method leaves survivors and a seed bank; mechanical treatments in the North Concho program missed 40 to 100 small plants per acre. "Without follow-up, initial brush control is expected to have a 20 to 30 year life. In contrast, with properly applied low cost follow-up treatments, an expensive initial treatment will last indefinitely," with the first follow-up due three to five years after the initial job.[^17] In the south Texas analysis Hamilton and Conner cite, programs with maintenance burning averaged an 11.1 percent internal rate of return against 2.4 percent without it.[^13] Recent field trials put the life of a standard mesquite herbicide program at 8.6 years, with follow-up costing about $109 per hectare, roughly $44 an acre, in 2016 dollars.[^18] A [monitoring flight](/services/monitoring/) a few years after treatment tells you when that bill is coming, before it gets big.

## Cost-share: what the programs actually fund

Two programs matter in West Texas.

The Texas State Soil and Water Conservation Board's Water Supply Enhancement Program, created by the Legislature in 2011, cost-shares brush control for water yield, not forage. It targets juniper, mesquite and saltcedar, pays up to 70 percent of the cost of a practice, and requires a 10-year resource management plan that spells out follow-up treatments and the brush density you will maintain. Money goes through a competitive grant process ranked by projected water yield in designated watersheds.[^19]

USDA's Environmental Quality Incentives Program is the general one. NRCS runs it through county offices under practice standard 314, Brush Management. The Extension economist's description of the Texas program lists cost-share rates generally in the 40 to 50 percent range, some practices as high as 75 percent, with higher rates for limited-resource producers. It has funded follow-up as well as initial control.[^8] Rates and ranking change every fiscal year, so the current numbers come from your NRCS field office, not from this article.

Remember the Edwards Plateau result: three of four regions lost money without a cost share.[^12] On a lot of Texas country, cost share is the difference between profitable and not.

## Hunting, water and land value

Teague's analysis is the clearest on the other income lines. The baseline assumed $3 an acre a year in wildlife income. If treatment cut that to $1, net present value went negative for both fire and herbicide. If it raised it to $5, NPV rose to $39 and $44 an acre. Wildlife generally wants 20 to 30 percent mesquite cover, and clearing too much can cost lease income.[^7] Texas Parks and Wildlife's short-term lease program paid landowners about $2 to $10 an acre in 2012 and says areas "with high quality managed habitat" get the best price.[^20] On our plans, oaks and piñons are not protected by default. The owner marks the trees you want kept.

Water is the line most often oversold. Wilcox's review concluded there is "little if any real potential for increasing streamflow where annual precipitation is below about 500 mm," about 20 inches; that clearing upland mesquite is unlikely to change streamflow; and that the exception is cedar on shallow soils over fractured rock, where springs are the sign to look for.[^21] The Texas Water Resources Institute's review reached the same place: gains where the canopy is dense and the geology lets water move, little otherwise, and none if the brush is allowed back.[^22] Where it works, the public cost of added water was put at $26 to $44 per thousand cubic meters in the Edwards Aquifer recharge zone against $51 to $129 at Twin Buttes.[^23]

On land value, the economic studies leave it out. Teague's group excluded it because land values "are often unrelated to productivity and are frequently driven by speculative investors."[^7] We found no reputable Texas study that isolates the effect of brush control on sale price, so we will not claim one.

## Where brush control does not pay

Four cases from the literature.

Low-rainfall country responds slowly. Most of the numbers above come from 20 to 26 inch country.[^6][^21] Ansley's 20-year record showed brush cover rising fastest in a run of wet years, and the 1977 to 1981 drought killing large stands of buffalograss outright.[^6] For the Trans-Pecos, Texas Parks and Wildlife says that in many areas "a major reclamation program involving brush control and grazing deferment would be required to partially restore desert grasslands."[^10]

Drought can erase a year of response. Teague's model delays the forage response a full year after a burn for that reason, and warns that in dry years burning may be impossible.[^7] The 2021 Rolling Plains study is the mirror image: too much rain in the wrong season turned a grass response into a forb response.[^5]

Dense, old stands are the expensive ones. The reclamation treatment a 60 percent cedar canopy needs is the one whose life "is usually not sufficient to recover treatment costs."[^15]

And the usual mistake is the acreage guess. Every model above starts from a brush density, and the Extension economist who built the North Concho estimates calls grazing capacity "the cornerstone" of the calculation.[^8] A plan that says "about 2,000 acres of medium mesquite" has no density in it and cannot be run through any of them.[^13][^15]
