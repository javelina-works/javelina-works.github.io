Most of the brush questions we get come down to the same thing: what does the chemical actually do, and is it the right tool for this piece of country. This is a plain guide to the herbicides used on Texas rangeland. It is not a pitch for one product or one method. Throughout, "cedar" means what it means on a West Texas ranch: redberry juniper and Ashe juniper.

## Two ways to kill a woody plant

Two modes of action do almost all the brush work on Texas rangeland.[^1]

The growth regulators, or synthetic auxins (Group 4), are the most common. They disrupt the hormone processes that govern cell division and growth and set off rapid, uncontrolled growth at the cellular level until the plant dies.[^1] 2,4-D, triclopyr, picloram, clopyralid, aminopyralid and fluroxypyr are all in this group.[^1] They are usually sprayed on leaves or stems, though picloram also works on the soil.[^5]

The photosynthesis inhibitors (Groups 5 and 7) are hexazinone and tebuthiuron. They block the plant from turning sunlight into usable energy, and it dies as reactive molecules build up.[^1] Both go on the soil and are taken up by roots after rain moves them into the root zone.[^8] EPA calls tebuthiuron "a relatively nonselective, soil activated herbicide."[^2] Hexazinone is also non-selective and generally kills all vegetation where it is immediately applied,[^3] which is why both are usually put down in measured doses under individual plants.

## Which product goes on which brush

| Active ingredient                 | Example product           | Target brush                           | Method                                 | Timing                                                |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Aminopyralid + clopyralid         | Sendero                   | Mesquite                               | Leaf spray, 1 percent in water         | Dark-green leaves, soil 75°F at 12 inches[^5]         |
| Triclopyr ester                   | Remedy Ultra              | Mesquite                               | Stem spray, 15 to 25 percent in diesel | Any time, best in growing season[^4]                  |
| Triclopyr ester or picloram       | Remedy Ultra, Tordon 22K  | Mesquite, redberry cedar               | Cut stump                              | Any time the stump can be sprayed to ground line[^5]  |
| Hexazinone                        | Velpar L, Pronone pellets | Redberry and Ashe cedar                | Soil spot, liquid or pellets           | Late winter through summer, ahead of rain[^5][^11]    |
| Tebuthiuron                       | Spike 20P                 | Catclaw, whitebrush, creosote, tarbush | Broadcast or spot pellets              | Any time; Davis Mountains optimum May 1 to July 1[^5] |
| Picloram or picloram + fluroxypyr | Tordon 22K, Surmount      | Pricklypear                            | Pad spray, 1 percent in water          | Any time, best late summer to fall[^5]                |

Mesquite is a leaf-spray plant. The Brush Busters method wets all the leaves of a mesquite under 6 feet with 1 percent aminopyralid plus clopyralid (Sendero, for example) or 0.5 percent triclopyr plus 0.5 percent clopyralid.[^4] Texas A&M's 2026 _Chemical Weed and Brush Control_ guide (ERM-1466) rates that individual plant treatment Very High and the same mix broadcast at 28 ounces per acre High.[^5] The stem spray is for mesquite with three or fewer smooth stems: triclopyr ester at 15 percent in diesel for stems 1.5 inches or less, 25 percent for larger or rough-barked stems, from the ground up 12 inches.[^4] Cut stumps get triclopyr in oil on the root collar, sides and cambium,[^6] or, for redberry cedar, picloram at 4 percent in water.[^5]

Cedar is a soil-treatment plant. For redberry juniper under 6 feet, ERM-1466 suggests undiluted hexazinone liquid at 2 milliliters per 3 feet of height or canopy diameter, whichever is greater, or 1 hexazinone pellet per 3 feet, rated Very High; over 6 feet the dose doubles and the rating drops to High.[^5] Ashe juniper gets the same dose, and undiluted picloram at 4 milliliters per 3 feet is the other soil option.[^5] None of these go on marshy, poorly drained or clay soils; the best results come on coarse-textured ground.[^5]

Tebuthiuron (Spike 20P, for example) is the broadcast pellet. ERM-1466 lists it for Davis Mountains mixed brush, meaning catclaw acacia, catclaw mimosa and whitebrush, at 7.5 to 10 pounds of pellets per acre, rated Moderate broadcast and High as a spot treatment.[^5] It gives little control of mesquite or pricklypear.[^7]

Pricklypear is a picloram plant. ERM-1466 rates picloram at 1 percent on individual pads Very High and 16 to 32 ounces per acre broadcast High, best late summer through fall.[^5] The herbicide is taken up through the pads and through the roots after rain, and total kill can take two or three years.[^9]

## Timing is most of the job

Mesquite leaf sprays only work in a narrow window. Brush Busters says to begin when the leaves turn from light pea-green to a uniform dark green, continue through July 31 in East and South Texas and possibly through September elsewhere, and never spray wet leaves, damaged foliage or new growth flushed by rain.[^4] The soil at 12 to 18 inches should have reached 75 degrees.[^10] ERM-1466 says the optimum period begins when the 12-inch soil temperature reaches 75°F and runs 45 days.[^5] For western honey mesquite, the form most common in the western Trans-Pecos, the window is 40 to 90 days after bud break, and the guide warns that not all years provide adequate conditions.[^5] Top-killed mesquite needs two full growing seasons of regrowth before a leaf spray.[^4]

Soil treatments depend on rain, not leaves. The Pronone label says moisture is required to activate the pellets, best results come when a quarter to half inch of rain falls within two weeks, and woody plants usually respond within three to six weeks after enough rain has carried the herbicide into the root zone.[^11] The Spike label says symptoms appear fastest when pellets go down just before seasonal rain, and control may take from one to several years.[^8] Velpar's label says that in the West, results may take one to two years in low-rainfall areas.[^23]

## What the control numbers mean

ERM-1466 rates every treatment on a four-step scale: Very High is 76 to 100 percent of plants killed, High is 56 to 75, Moderate is 36 to 55 and Low is 0 to 35, when the treatment is applied properly under optimum conditions.[^5] It also says plainly that "seldom is a species eradicated."[^5]

Field numbers show how much the site matters. A Texas Cooperative Extension demonstration put hexazinone pellets, Velpar L and Tordon 22K on redberry and Ashe juniper at five sites in 2001. Two years later, root kill with pellets averaged 84 percent at 1 pellet per 3 feet and 94 percent at 2 pellets, Velpar averaged 88 and 96 percent, and there was no significant difference between herbicides or rates; but the 1-pellet rate ranged from 47 percent at one site to 100 percent at another.[^3] A 1972 Rolling Plains study reported over 95 percent control of redberry foliage within a year from soil-applied picloram.[^14] The pattern is consistent: small trees on coarse, shallow soil are the sure kills, and the odds fall as the tree gets bigger and the soil gets heavier.

## Why soil-applied pellets suit cedar

The two cedars die differently. Ashe juniper does not resprout; cutting it at or slightly above the ground kills it at any size.[^13] Redberry juniper resprouts from a bud zone at the base of the stem, which sits above ground for roughly the first seven years and is then buried, after which cutting or fire above it just produces a new crown.[^15] This is why Brush Busters says top removal controls blueberry (Ashe) cedar but redberry must be grubbed below the soil surface.[^17]

A root-absorbed herbicide gets around that. The plant takes it up with soil water, and the demonstrations above measured root kill, not top kill, which is the number that matters for a sprouter.[^3] The Pronone label puts the redberry dose at 1 to 2 pellets per 3 feet of height or canopy diameter, placed about halfway between the stem and the canopy edge.[^11] A pelleted herbicide has fewer timing constraints and little drift, but the plant dies slowly over one to three years, often releafing before it goes, and leaves a small dead circle of grass under it for a year or more.[^7] Hexazinone pellets are what we use for individual plant treatment of cedar from the air.

## The label is the law

Every pesticide label carries the line "It is a violation of Federal law to use this product in a manner inconsistent with its labeling," and EPA treats the label as legally enforceable.[^18] In Texas a product also has to be approved by the Texas Department of Agriculture.[^5]

Who can apply what. Picloram products (Tordon 22K and Surmount, for example) are Restricted Use, applied only by or under the supervision of certified applicators.[^19] Texas A&M's guide lists 2,4-D and dicamba products as needing a license to buy, while Sendero, Remedy Ultra, Velpar and Spike 20P do not.[^1] A rancher using restricted-use pesticides or regulated herbicides on his own land needs a TDA private applicator license: a training course, an exam, a five-year term and 15 continuing education credits to renew.[^20] Applying them on someone else's land for hire takes a commercial applicator license.[^21]

Grazing and haying. Sendero has no grazing or grass-hay restriction, but the label says to wait 14 days to cut hay and warns that urine and manure from cattle on treated grass can carry enough aminopyralid to injure sensitive broadleaf plants.[^22] Remedy Ultra restricts only lactating dairy animals, hay waits 14 days, and livestock come off treated grass three days before slaughter.[^6] Spike 20P has no grazing restriction but no hay for one year.[^8] The Spike figures here come from the 2006 label, and labels change, so the one on the container you buy is the one that counts. Pronone pellets at rangeland rates below 1 pound of product per acre carry no grazing or hay restriction.[^11]

Drift. The user is responsible for drift onto a neighbor's property.[^5] Pellets do not drift in the air, but they move with water: the Spike label says not to apply where overland flow could carry pellets off the site, and once rain has moved the herbicide into the root zone the effect is irreversible.[^8]

Groundwater. EPA requires every hexazinone label to carry an advisory that it "is known to leach through soil into ground water under certain conditions," especially on permeable soils over a shallow water table; EPA had found it in groundwater in four states, well below the 200 parts per billion drinking water advisory.[^24] Tebuthiuron carries the same advisory, with detections in Texas and California and a shallow water table defined as 30 feet or less.[^2] Picloram is "among the most mobile of currently registered pesticides," with soil half-lives of 167 to 513 days.[^19] New Mexico State adds a rule for Velpar L: treat no more than 25 percent of a watershed.[^12] Aminopyralid sits at the other end: EPA registered it in 2005 as a Reduced Risk herbicide, with field half-lives of 20 and 32 days.[^25]

Non-target trees. Soil herbicides do not know a cedar root from an oak root. The Pronone label says to measure from the trunk of a desirable tree to its dripline, multiply by three, and keep pellets outside that distance.[^11] The Spike label warns that "exposure of even a small part of a plant root system to Spike 20P may cause severe plant injury or death."[^8] Even the Sendero label says trees next to a treated area can be affected by root uptake.[^22] On a Javelina Works survey map, oaks and piñons are not protected by default; the owner marks the trees you want kept.

## How chemical compares with a dozer or a burn

Texas A&M's brush methods bulletin draws the line at the roots: methods that remove only top growth give short-term control because most species resprout, while methods that take part of the root system last longer; its table gives grubbing five years or more on non-sprouters and bulldozing two to three years with soil disturbance.[^7] Ueckert's juniper review says maintenance control is always necessary within a few years after mechanical methods because of resprouting and rapid growth.[^13]

Fire is the cheapest tool and the most fuel-hungry. The bulletin lists minimal soil disturbance and lower cost among its advantages, but says 2,500 to 3,000 pounds per acre of fine fuel is generally needed and grazing deferment the season before is normally required.[^7] Ashe juniper is most vulnerable in its first 10 years and under 10 feet; redberry is easier under 40 inches, while the bud zone is above ground, with roughly 2,000 pounds per acre of fine fuel giving 70 to 75 percent mortality; and a 5 to 7 year fire return interval should keep new cedar from establishing.[^15]

Chemical fills the gap on scattered brush and bigger trees. Individual plant treatment is usually more effective than broadcast with the same herbicide and is best suited to thin stands and maintenance after a broadcast job; some foliar treatments regress to pre-treatment forage production in three to five years, while some soil-applied treatments have a projected life of over 20 years.[^7] The money side is in [what brush control does for a cattle operation](/articles/brush-control-cattle-profitability/).

## Follow-up and maintenance

No treatment is a one-time job. Brush Busters says livestock and wildlife spread mesquite seed and the land needs checking every year.[^4] Redberry seed is spread by birds, coyotes, foxes, raccoons, rabbits, deer and livestock.[^16] The Pronone label says not to cut pellet-treated brush for 12 months, not to burn it once it browns, and to dose resprouts for the original tree, not the sprout.[^11] Spike says not to cut, chain or burn treated plants for two years.[^8] The useful habit is a baseline count, so you know what the treatment did and what is coming back, which is the point of a [survey that counts every woody plant](/services/survey-work/).
