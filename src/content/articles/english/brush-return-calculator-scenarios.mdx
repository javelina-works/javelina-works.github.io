Our [brush return calculator](/tools/brush-roi/) takes a ranch, a kind of brush and a cover target, and works out what clearing could give back in grass, cattle, water, fire behavior and wildlife cover. It opens with four example ranches. They are not there to sell anything. They show how differently the same job pays depending on the soil, the grass, the rainfall and how far the brush has already gone.

All four use the same defaults. Harvest efficiency is 25 percent, the "take half, leave half" rate most ranchers should aim for once trampling, insects and wildlife take their share.[^1] Grazing is priced at $14.50 per animal unit month, the 2024 Texas average for private grazing.[^2] Future value is discounted at 5.25 percent over 20 years, the brush left alone spreads one point of canopy a year, and the year is a normal one. Site potential comes from NRCS soil survey data for Jeff Davis County.[^3]

## 1. Mesquite flat, Jeff Davis County

A section, 640 acres, of Loamy Desert Grassland at 16 inches of rain. In a normal year the site can grow 1,034 pounds of forage an acre, mostly grama shortgrass. Mesquite covers 25 percent and gets thinned to 5.

| What changes              | Today | After               |
| ------------------------- | ----- | ------------------- |
| Grass, lb/acre            | 863   | 1,000 (+137)        |
| Acres per animal unit     | 44    | 38                  |
| Animal units on the place | 14.5  | 16.9 (+2.3)         |
| Fire                      | High  | Low                 |
| Wildlife cover fits       |       | Pronghorn, bobwhite |

That is about 28 more animal unit months a year, worth about $403. Over 20 years, discounted, the grazing gain comes to $6,603, or $10.32 an acre. About $3.35 of that is grass you keep by stopping the mesquite from spreading further.

The grass gain is small because shortgrass sites don't lose much to mesquite in the first place. In a Texas mesquite savanna, going from no mesquite to 35 percent cover cut shortgrass production by 23 percent and warm-season midgrass by 76 percent.[^4] Grama on a loamy flat holds on under mesquite. That makes it a poor site for a grazing-only argument.

So what carries the case here? Three things. Cost share: the FY2025 Texas EQIP payment for individual plant treatment at 50 to 200 plants an acre was $21.30 an acre,[^5] about double this site's grazing break-even. Whether a contract is available and which practice scenario applies is up to your NRCS field office, but that is why cost share decides this kind of job. Drought flexibility: 2.3 more animal units of carrying capacity is room you don't have to buy hay for in a dry year. And acting early, before the stand thickens and moves into a costlier treatment class. The [paying for brush work](/articles/paying-for-brush-work-trans-pecos/) article covers the programs.

On water, the brush stops using about 0.6 inches a year, roughly 17,400 gallons an acre or 11.1 million gallons across the section. That water stays in the soil, and it is the water that grows the extra grass. Creeks and springs: likely none. More on why below.

## 2. Juniper on an igneous slope, Davis Mountains

1,200 acres of Igneous Hill and Mountain, Mixed Prairie, at 19 inches. This is better country: 2,048 pounds an acre of potential in a normal year, with midgrasses. Juniper covers 35 percent and gets cut to 10. Because the ground is steep and rocky, the calculator counts only 60 percent of it as grazable.

| What changes              | Today     | After                             |
| ------------------------- | --------- | --------------------------------- |
| Grass, lb/acre            | 1,193     | 1,893 (+700)                      |
| Acres per animal unit     | 53        | 33                                |
| Animal units on the place | 22.6      | 35.9 (+13.3)                      |
| Fire                      | Very high | Moderate                          |
| Wildlife cover fits       |           | Pronghorn, bobwhite, scaled quail |

The 700-pound gain is a ceiling, not the raw curve. The redberry juniper production curve says this site would gain more,[^6] but the largest gain measured after near-complete brush kill in Trans-Pecos and Chihuahuan Desert trials was about 700 pounds an acre, near Fort Stockton.[^7] The calculator won't promise more than that.

That is about 159 more animal unit months a year, worth about $2,313. Over 20 years the grazing gain is $38,502, or $32.09 an acre, of which about $10.19 is grass you'd lose as the juniper kept spreading.

Fire is where this site changes most. Under wildfire winds in a Nebraska study, modeled embers from juniper woodland carried about five times farther than from grassland,[^8] and grass fires burn with shorter flames that crews can catch. The calculator shows that as a tier, very high to moderate, and the [wildfire article](/articles/brush-and-wildfire-davis-mountains/) goes into it.

Water is the most uncertain number here. The brush stops using about 1.4 inches, roughly 38,700 gallons an acre. For creeks and springs the calculator says borderline. Nineteen inches clears the 18-inch screen the state uses to site brush-for-water projects,[^9] but sits under the roughly 20 inches below which researchers see "little if any real potential" for more streamflow.[^10] Expect somewhere between zero and 17,300 gallons an acre, the average a USGS model found in a basin near Lubbock that gets 16 to 20 inches,[^11] and more only where a spring shows the water has a way down through fractured rock.

The trade-off worth seeing on this site is deer. Ten percent cover fits pronghorn and quail, but mule deer do best at 15 to 40 percent woody cover.[^12] Change "what you keep" to 20 percent in the [calculator](/tools/brush-roi/) and mule deer join the list. Grass after treatment comes back to about 1,717 pounds instead of 1,893, a quarter less gain, and the 20-year value drops from $32.09 to $26.57 an acre. The herd it supports grows by about 9.9 animal units instead of 13.3. Either way it is a choice you make on purpose, and the [how much brush to keep](/articles/how-much-brush-to-keep/) article walks through it.

## 3. Catch it early

1,000 acres of Clay Loam, Mixed Prairie, at 16 inches: 1,800 pounds an acre of potential, midgrass. Mesquite is only at 10 percent and gets taken to 2.

| What changes              | Today    | After        |
| ------------------------- | -------- | ------------ |
| Grass, lb/acre            | 1,412    | 1,722 (+311) |
| Acres per animal unit     | 27       | 22           |
| Animal units on the place | 37.2     | 45.4 (+8.2)  |
| Fire                      | Moderate | Low          |

The 20-year grazing value is $34,162, or $34.16 an acre, the highest per acre of the three Trans-Pecos examples. And $19.00 of that is grass you would lose as the brush spreads, not grass you get back.

Two lessons. First, midgrass sites lose more to even light mesquite, so 10 percent cover is already costing 388 pounds an acre here. Second, most of the value is in not letting it go further. Range researchers working on juniper reached the same advice: grass declines slowly at first and fast between about 19 and 29 percent cover, so treat before cover passes about 12 percent.[^6]

Waiting also costs on the treatment side. NRCS sets its payments by stand density. Working back from the FY2025 payment rates, the cost NRCS assumes for treatment rises from about $28 an acre for individual plant treatment at 50 to 200 plants, to about $69 at 201 to 400 plants, to about $272 for mechanical work at 31 to 50 percent canopy.[^5] Those are the agency's cost assumptions, not anyone's quote, but they show how fast the bill climbs as a stand thickens.

## 4. Hill Country cedar, for comparison

500 acres on the Edwards Plateau at 26 inches, over karst, with juniper at 40 percent cut to 10. Site potential is 1,151 pounds an acre, from the same juniper research.[^6]

| What changes              | Today     | After        |
| ------------------------- | --------- | ------------ |
| Grass, lb/acre            | 568       | 1,101 (+533) |
| Acres per animal unit     | 67        | 34           |
| Animal units on the place | 7.5       | 14.5 (+7.0)  |
| Fire                      | Very high | Moderate     |

The 20-year grazing value is $18,319, or $36.64 an acre. The brush stops using about 2.3 inches, roughly 63,500 gallons an acre. And here the calculator says something it says nowhere in the Trans-Pecos: springs and aquifer could get 1 to 4 acre-inches an acre a year, 27,154 to 108,616 gallons.[^13]

This ranch is in the tool because that is where the big water numbers come from. The studies behind them were done on wetter karst with dense cedar. They do not transfer to 16-inch country, where the water a dead mesquite no longer uses "may simply allow more water to be stored in the soil for use by the remaining grasses and forbs."[^13] The [brush and water](/articles/brush-and-water-dry-country/) article explains the difference.

## How to read the calculator

**Break-even or your quote.** Leave the cost box blank and the [calculator](/tools/brush-roi/) gives a break-even: the most you could spend per acre, after cost share, and get it back from extra grazing alone. Enter a quote and it shows the year grazing pays it back, if it does inside 20 years.

**Water is two numbers on purpose.** The first is water the brush stops using. Most of it stays in your soil and grows the grass already counted under cattle, so never add its value to the grazing value. The second is water that might reach creeks, springs or an aquifer. That one is a published range, not a calculation, and below 18 inches it defaults to "likely none."[^9][^10]

**Fire is a tier, not dollars.** Brush changes how a fire behaves: how hot, how far embers fly, whether crews can hold it. It does not tell you whether you'll have a fire, and a federal fuel-treatment study found median suppression savings of zero because fires so rarely meet treated ground.[^14] A dollar figure for avoided loss would be invented.

**Wildlife bands.** These are Texas Parks and Wildlife's woody-cover targets for the Trans-Pecos: pronghorn 1 to 10 percent, bobwhite 5 to 25, scaled quail 10 to 35, mule deer 15 to 40.[^12] TPWD's own words: "inadequate cover can be just as detrimental as too much brush."[^12]

**Wildlife valuation.** If your land is under wildlife management use, brush management counts as a qualifying practice in the Trans-Pecos when it covers at least 10 percent of the plan area or 100 acres a year, whichever is smaller.[^15] The calculator shows that number for your acreage. It is a valuation, not an exemption, and your appraisal district decides.

## What it can't tell you

These are planning numbers from public research, not a promise. The forage response comes from studies of other treatments; we found no published forage-response data yet for drone-applied hexazinone pellets. Treatment life is borrowed from root-killing mesquite herbicide work, which puts it at 15 to 20 years.[^16] The defaults use county-weighted soil values, and your own site's NRCS soil survey value is better: put it in the custom box. If you don't know your brush cover or density, the [ballpark tool](/tools/ballpark/) gives a first read from public imagery, and a survey flight replaces the assumptions with counts of every plant.

The four ranches land in different places for plain reasons. Shortgrass flats gain less than midgrass. Juniper hill country gains the most grass and changes most under fire. Light, early brush is mostly about what you avoid. And the large water numbers belong to wetter country. For the published research on whether brush control pays, see [what brush control does for profitability](/articles/brush-control-cattle-profitability/). For your own place, [run the calculator](/tools/brush-roi/) with your acres, your soil and your cover.
