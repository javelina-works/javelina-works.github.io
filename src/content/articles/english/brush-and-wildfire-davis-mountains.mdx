The [cedar and wildfire section](/articles/why-brush-control/#cedar-and-wildfire) of our long brush article covers the basics: grass fire kills small cedar, big cedar is a ladder, and redberry juniper comes back from the base. This piece goes further. It covers what dense juniper does to a fire on a bad day, why mesquite is a different problem, and the parts of the story that cut the other way. Then it gets to the practical part: what you can do around your house, your roads and your pastures.

Start with one rule. Brush changes how fire behaves. It does not decide whether you get a fire, and nobody can honestly tell you how many dollars of loss a brush job will save you.

## The fire everyone here remembers

The Rock House fire was first reported on April 9, 2011. It burned about 314,000 acres, per NASA,[^1] most of it on the east side of Jeff Davis County, and burned until May 12.[^2] It destroyed 24 homes in Fort Davis[^3] and threatened McDonald Observatory[^2] and Fort Davis National Historic Site.[^4]

We are not going to tell you brush caused that fire or made it worse. We have not seen an after-action report that describes the fuels it burned through. What Rock House does show is that this country can carry a very large, fast fire into town. The question for a landowner is what the vegetation on your place does once a fire like that arrives.

## What juniper does to a fire

Grass fire and cedar fire are not the same fire. Researchers studying Ashe juniper at four central Texas sites over eight years used two trigger points for the trees' live fuel moisture: below 100 percent, single trees torching becomes common in moderate fires, and below 80 percent, crown fire running tree to tree becomes a real possibility. They found those low moisture levels got more likely once the Keetch-Byram Drought Index climbed above about 500.[^5] In practice: the deeper the drought, the closer your cedar is to torching.

Oklahoma State's extension service describes eastern redcedar the same way: in drought and extreme weather it can drive "erratic to extreme fire behavior that is resistant to control," with taller flames, more firebrands, and ladder fuels that carry a surface fire up into the crowns.[^6]

Embers are the other half. A Nebraska team modeled spot fires across a landscape turning from grassland to juniper. Under wildfire winds, embers from grassland did not land more than about 1.4 kilometers out. Embers from juniper woodland reached nearly 7 kilometers, about five times farther, and exposed roughly ten times as much fuel to new ignitions.[^7] That is a model, and it is eastern redcedar in Nebraska, not juniper in the Davis Mountains. But it shows how a cedar slope a few miles off can put fire on your side of a road.

Flame length is what decides whether a fire can be caught. Standard suppression guidance says hand crews can attack flames under about 4 feet directly and expect a hand line to hold. Between 4 and 8 feet, it takes dozers, engines or aircraft.[^8] Grass fuels tend to burn with shorter flames that crews can work, while volatile woody fuels like dense cedar burn hotter and are harder to suppress.[^7]

## Mesquite comes back, most cedar does not

Brush does not all respond to fire the same way, and it matters for what a fire leaves behind.

Ashe juniper does not resprout. After crown fires in oak-juniper woodland it stayed largely absent for decades.[^9] The fire-response research we found is on Ashe juniper. We did not find the same work for one-seed or the other junipers on Davis Mountains slopes, so treat any comparison there as less certain.

Mesquite is the opposite. In one set of high-intensity fires, 100 percent of the mesquite was top-killed, and only 29 percent failed to come back.[^10] Texas Parks and Wildlife puts it plainly for this part of the state: mesquite, redberry juniper, creosote and tarbush "are difficult to kill with fire. They generally will resprout from the roots." A hot fire can kill mesquite seedlings under about 3 years old and juniper seedlings under about 8.[^11] So a wildfire through mesquite country usually hands you back the same mesquite, multi-stemmed and resprouting. We also found no study measuring mesquite's spotting distance or flame length like the juniper work above, so the strong fire claims here are about cedar.

That split is part of why [individual plant treatment](/articles/individual-plant-treatment/) exists. Fire handles small cedar well and older resprouters poorly.

## The honest part: what clearing brush does not fix

This is where most brush pitches get loose. Here is the other side.

**Clearing grows grass, and grass burns.** Grass is fine fuel. It dries fast and a grass fire runs fast. The difference is that it burns with shorter flames and is easier for crews to put out than a fire in volatile woody fuel.[^7] You are trading a hotter, harder fire for a faster, lower one. That is usually a good trade, but it is not "less fire." The grass is also what makes prescribed fire possible, and one extension guide calls fire the only tool that can manage encroachment across a whole landscape.[^12]

**Grass fires can still be catastrophic.** The 2024 Smokehouse Creek fire in the Panhandle burned about 1.075 million acres, the largest wildfire in Texas history.[^13] Under extreme wind, open country burns hard too.

**Dead trees stay fuel for a while.** Herbicide kills a tree where it stands, and Texas Parks and Wildlife notes that herbicides "leave the dead standing remains," which some wildlife use.[^11] We did not find any study measuring how long a standing dead juniper or mesquite stays a fire hazard. Assume it is still fuel for some years, and plan around it near anything you care about.

**The money is lopsided.** Forest Service modeling of fuel treatments found that because fires rarely hit treated ground, "median suppression cost savings are zero." In the rare extreme year, savings can more than pay for the treatment.[^15] That work was in western forests, not rangeland, but the logic carries over. Fire value from brush work is a smaller downside on your worst day. It is not a check you can count on, which is why we do not put a fire dollar figure on it.

## What is at stake on a ranch

The best accounting of what a big fire costs a ranch comes from the Panhandle. Texas A&M AgriLife economists put preliminary agricultural losses from the 2024 fires at $123 million or more across more than 1.2 million acres.[^14] By category:

- About $68.7 million in ranch infrastructure: fences, barns, corrals, well pump motors, windmills, and hay and feed.
- About $27 million in cattle, more than 12,000 head.
- About $26 million in lost grazing and emergency feeding.
- About $1 million in other costs, such as carcass disposal and forced sales.

Most of that loss is fence, water and feed, the things a ranch runs on. It is a useful list to walk through on your own place: how many miles of fence, which wells and windmills, where the hay sits, and where the cattle would be on a red-flag afternoon. Brush work does not protect any of it by itself. It changes what a fire looks like when it gets there.

## What you can actually do

**Start at the house.** Defensible space guidance for rural Texas breaks it into zones. Within 5 feet, remove dead plants and anything flammable. From 5 to 30 feet, space shrubs and trees apart and keep grass mowed. From 30 to 100 feet, thin trees, cut back dense brush and keep access roads open. The home ignition zone can reach out to 200 feet.[^16] If cedar is touching the eaves, nothing else on this list matters as much.

**Break up fuel along roads and lines.** A strip of short, open ground along a ranch road, a fence line or a power line gives a fire a place to lie down and gives crews somewhere to work from. NRCS has a conservation practice for exactly this, Fuel Break (383), and it is often paired with Prescribed Burning (338) and Woody Residue Treatment (384).[^17] Treating the cedar thickets next to your main roads also keeps them open for getting out and for engines coming in.

**Use fire while it is your choice.** Under prescribed-burn weather, the Nebraska model's spotting distances were two to three times shorter than under wildfire winds.[^7] Burning on your terms is a different fire from the one that finds you in April. Prescribed fire works best on young, small cedar, and AgriLife's general recommendation is about 2,000 pounds per acre of fine fuel to carry it.[^18] Where grazing or limited rain keeps grass below that, fire is harder to use,[^18] and plenty of Trans-Pecos country is short on grass. Texas Parks and Wildlife says West Texas rangeland does not need burning more often than every 8 to 12 years.[^11]

You do not have to do it alone. The Trans-Pecos Prescribed Burn Association has operated since 2009, one of several Texas associations where landowners pool equipment, labor and experience. Texas also had 528 commercial and 106 private certified and insured burn managers in 2022.[^19]

**Look at the funding before you spend.** Texas A&M Forest Service offers prescribed fire grants statewide, at $22.50 to $30 per acre in its 2026 round. Its mechanical fuel reduction grants, the ones that pay for cutting and mulching, cover only 39 Central Texas counties.[^20] If your neighbors are organized, the federal Community Wildfire Defense Grant program funds community wildfire protection plans (up to $250,000) and fuel reduction projects (up to $10 million). Local governments, nonprofits and similar groups apply, not individual landowners.[^21] Texas A&M Forest Service's risk portal, TxWRAP, lets you look up wildfire threat and past fire activity for your area.[^22] For brush cost-share more broadly, see [paying for brush work in the Trans-Pecos](/articles/paying-for-brush-work-trans-pecos/).

## Putting it together

On a ranch where dense cedar has filled in the slopes, pulling it back toward scattered trees and grass moves your fire toward something a crew can catch: lower flames, fewer embers, less torching. On a mesquite flat, the fire case is weaker, and grass and grazing carry the argument. That case is laid out in [why brush control](/articles/why-brush-control/) and in the [brush return calculator](/tools/brush-roi/). Either way, you are deciding [how much brush to keep](/articles/how-much-brush-to-keep/), not whether to clear it all. Cover for deer and quail matters too (see [valuing wildlife in brush management](/articles/wildlife-valuation-brush-management/)).

If you want to know how much cedar is close to your house, roads and lines, the [instant ballpark](/tools/ballpark/) reads your ranch from public imagery in about a minute, free. A plant-by-plant survey maps every tree, so you can decide where the fuel comes out and what stays.
