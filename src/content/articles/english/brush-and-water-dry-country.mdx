Almost everyone who calls about brush asks some version of this: if I kill the mesquite and the cedar, will the creek run again? Will the spring come back? Water and forage are the two reasons Texas landowners most often give for wanting less brush.[^1] So the question deserves a straight answer, and in most of the Trans-Pecos the straight answer is no, not in the creek. The water does go somewhere, though, and where it goes is worth understanding.

Our [research overview](/articles/why-brush-control/) covers water in a few paragraphs alongside forage, wildlife and fire. This article stays on water and on our country: what the big numbers are based on, why they don't carry over to 16 inches of rain, and how to think about it on your own place.

## Where the big numbers come from

The tens-of-thousands-of-gallons figures are real. They just come from somewhere else.

In 2000 the state funded brush-control feasibility studies on a set of Texas watersheds. Computer models of those basins, simulating removal of all heavy and moderate brush, predicted water gains of about 6,650 to 172,000 gallons per treated acre per year.[^2] The state's Water Supply Enhancement Program later projected about 0.3 acre-feet, roughly 100,000 gallons, for each acre it paid to treat.[^3] None of those watersheds is in the Trans-Pecos.[^4]

The best field evidence comes from the Edwards Plateau, the Hill Country. There, dense Ashe juniper grows on thin soil over cracked limestone, and rain that gets past the roots can drop into the rock and come out in a spring. A Texas Water Resources Institute review concluded that clearing dense juniper and live oak in that kind of country "can produce 1 to 4 acre-inches of additional water per year for each acre of brush cleared."[^5] That is about 27,000 to 109,000 gallons. It also requires keeping the regrowth controlled and managing the grass that comes back.[^5]

Those sites get 20 inches of rain or more. Near Sonora, the plots that showed big gains in deep drainage averaged 22.6 inches over the study.[^5] The same state modeling that produced the big numbers found "little increase in streamflow" where rainfall averages under about 18 inches, and gains of about 2 and 4 inches a year only at 24 and 30 inches of rain.[^5]

## The 18-inch line, and where we sit

Two thresholds come up again and again in this research.

The State Soil and Water Conservation Board uses at least 18 inches of rain a year as its criterion for where brush control is worth doing for water.[^6] Brad Wilcox, a Texas A&M hydrologist who has spent decades on this question, put the floor a little higher: "little if any real potential for increasing streamflow where annual precipitation is below about 500 mm," which is about 20 inches.[^7]

Fort Davis averages about 16 inches. Marfa gets about 15.8 and Alpine about 15.[^8] Much of the lower country is drier still. Higher ground in the Davis Mountains gets more, and some of it may reach the 18 to 20 inch range, but no one has published a brush-and-water study there.

That is the gap. No state feasibility study, USGS model or field experiment covers Trans-Pecos uplands.[^4] The closest modeled case is Lake Alan Henry near Lubbock, with 16 to 20 inches of rain. There, USGS simulated an average of about 17,300 gallons per acre, ranging from 5,850 to 34,400 across sub-basins.[^9] That is a model of a different basin. When the state's regional water plans were last tallied, the Far West Texas planning group did not discuss brush control at all.[^3]

## What happened when Texas tried it at scale

The North Concho near San Angelo is the test case most people don't hear about. It gets around 21 to 22 inches of rain, more than any of us.[^10][^11] Between 2000 and 2005, about 432 of the watershed's 1,413 square miles were cleared of mesquite and juniper in a $14 million program. Planners projected stream flow would rise three- to five-fold. As of 2015 there had been "no perceptible increase."[^10]

The researchers gave several reasons. Potential evaporation at San Angelo is about six feet a year against about 21 inches of rain. Streams in western Texas run mostly on overland flow from big storms, not on groundwater. The models had little field validation and assumed plants used water at their summer peak all year. And planners assumed every brush plant would go, when landowners removed about half on average.[^10]

The savings on the ground were real, just small. Instruments on paired mesquite pastures measured roughly 1.8 inches less water used by the treated pasture over four years, about half an inch a year.[^11]

The Pecos tells the same story with saltcedar, the one brush species with field data from our part of the state. Saltcedar stands along the Pecos lost a lot of water, and herbicide treatment saved much of it at first: 82 percent two years after treatment, falling to 31 percent by year four as regrowth and other plants moved in.[^12] But near Mentone, after large stretches of saltcedar were killed, researchers found a weak response in one monitoring well and "no response in stream stage." Evaporation from the river channel itself was far larger than what the saltcedar was using.[^13] AgriLife also notes that the widely repeated claim of 200 gallons a day per saltcedar tree was "unsubstantiated" and later refuted.[^13]

## Balmorhea and Comanche Springs

Texas Parks and Wildlife attributes the decline of the Balmorhea-area springs and Comanche Springs at Fort Stockton to regional groundwater pumping. Its booklet on the desert spring fishes records that Comanche Springs first went dry in 1955 and is now almost always dry.[^14] Treating juniper in the mountains won't refill an aquifer that wells are drawing down.

## What the freed water actually does

When a mesquite dies, it stops pulling water out of the soil. In dry country that water mostly stays in the soil, and the grass uses it.

The Texas Water Resources Institute review puts it plainly: after mesquite control, the freed water "may simply allow more water to be stored in the soil for use by the remaining grasses and forbs." On a Throckmorton site with deep soil, killing mesquite did little to deep drainage or runoff over three years. The authors wrote that brush control there "may benefit the rancher by stimulating compensatory forage growth" but "does not necessarily increase streamflows or aquifer recharge."[^5]

The savings also shrink as the country recovers. On a Rolling Plains mesquite site, water use dropped by up to 40 percent right after treatment, but by the next summer the grass and forbs had come back and the reduction was 7 percent. At Seco Creek in the Hill Country, the cleared site used about 4.3 inches less water a year for two years, and by year three the two sites were about even as grass and other woody plants filled in.[^5] That is the grass taking the water, which is what you wanted.

Wilcox adds a point that matters a lot in drought country. Even where conditions are favorable, gains "will occur mainly during wet years and at relatively small scales; during dry years, and especially during droughts, it is doubtful that removal of shrub cover will affect streamflow."[^7]

### Interception is a gross number

Juniper does more than drink. Its canopy and litter catch rain before it reaches the ground. On the Edwards Plateau, interception by Ashe juniper ran about 2.4 inches a year at 20 percent canopy cover and up to 12.6 inches at full cover.[^15] That is the main reason juniper matters more for water than mesquite. Mesquite interception is probably about the same as grassland's.[^7]

But those inches are gross, not net. Once the juniper is gone, more rain reaches the soil, and grass and forbs use much of it. It tells you what the trees kept from the ground, not what will reach a spring.

## Don't multiply gallons per tree

You will see figures for how many gallons a juniper or mesquite uses per day, and it's tempting to multiply by trees per acre. Don't. The replacement vegetation uses a large share of what the brush gave up, so gallons per tree times trees per acre overstates the savings.[^5][^15]

## Where it might be different

There are two settings where the answer isn't "no."

The first is a spring or seep on high ground. Wilcox's main indicator for where brush control can affect water is "the presence of springs or groundwater flow to streams," usually where "soils are shallow and underlain by fractured parent material." Where there's no subsurface connection and runoff only comes as overland flow, "shrub control will have little if any influence on streamflow."[^7] Some higher-elevation Davis Mountains country, on thin soil over fractured volcanic rock with springs, at least resembles that description, and may get close to the rainfall line. That is our reading of the research, not a measured result. Nobody has data on it.

The second is riparian saltcedar over shallow groundwater. Studies on the Pecos estimated real water savings of about half an acre-foot to an acre-foot per acre treated. The researchers surmised that it went to groundwater recharge, not streamflow.[^15] That can matter to a shallow well, even if the river doesn't rise.

How the brush is killed also matters. In paired Arizona pinyon-juniper watersheds, streamflow rose where trees were killed by herbicide and left standing, but not where they were cabled or felled. The authors attributed this to undisturbed soil.[^15] Other studies found that when surface disturbance is minimal, grass replaces juniper quickly and runoff can actually drop.[^15] That's the case for methods that leave the ground alone, like [individual plant treatment](/articles/individual-plant-treatment/). It is an association from other places, not a promise for yours.

## Thinking about your own place

A few questions get you most of the way to an honest answer:

- **What does your ground actually get?** County averages hide a lot of elevation. Twelve inches on the flats and 19 on a mountain pasture are different questions.
- **Is there a spring, seep or shallow well, and what is it sitting in?** Thin soil over fractured rock is the setting where the research leaves the door open. Deep soil with no spring mostly means the water goes to grass.
- **What is the brush?** Dense evergreen juniper intercepts rain. Mesquite mostly competes for soil water that grass will take back.
- **Can you follow up?** Savings fade in two to three years as grass and leftover brush fill in, and partial removal gives less than the models assume.[^5][^10]
- **Are you counting the same water twice?** The water that stays in your soil is the water that grows the extra grass. If you're valuing the grass, as in [our cattle economics piece](/articles/brush-control-cattle-profitability/), don't add the water on top.

## How the calculator handles it

The [brush return calculator](/tools/brush-roi/) shows water as two separate numbers and never adds them to each other or to forage. The first is on-site water: the moisture brush no longer uses, which mostly stays in your soil and grows grass. The second is what might reach a creek, a spring or an aquifer, shown as the published range rather than a calculation. Under 18 inches of rain on upland ground it reads "likely none"; between 18 and 20 inches it reads borderline; fractured rock with springs, or a draw, gets its own range and caveats. Neither number counts the fade that comes without follow-up, so treat both as early-years figures. [Worked scenarios](/articles/brush-return-calculator-scenarios/) show how this plays out on a few typical places.
